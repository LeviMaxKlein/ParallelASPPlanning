\chapter*{Abstract}
Automated Planning is a fundamental concept in Artificial Intelligence that focuses on finding a sequence of actions to reach a desired goal from an initial state. Answer Set Programming (ASP) provides a powerful approach to tackle this challenge by modeling dynamic domains with concise declarative rules. However, traditional sequential encodings often suffer from a \mbox{search-depth} explosion due to long planning horizons. This thesis investigates the application of parallel planning techniques to overcome this bottleneck.
We first establish the theoretical foundations of planning tasks and define the semantics of sequential and parallel plans ($\forall$-step and $\exists$-step). Based on the framework proposed by Dimopoulos et al. (2019), specific ASP encodings for these planning strategies are presented.
The empirical evaluation demonstrates that parallel encodings significantly outperform the sequential baseline. Specifically, the encoding for $\forall$-step plans proves to be the most robust, solving a total of 448 out of 996 problem instances from a collection of IPC benchmark domains, whereas the sequential encoding solves only 202. Additionally, the inclusion of precomputed mutex groups improves performance in highly constrained domains like \textit{Agricola}, raising the total coverage to 457 instances while simultaneously reducing runtime for 417 instances. In contrast, neither the backward-propagating heuristic nor the guess-and-check strategy yielded significant performance improvements compared to the standard configuration, with guess-and-check solving only 366 instances.

\chapter{Introduction}\label{intro}


\section{Motivation}
A central objective of Artificial Intelligence is to equip systems with the ability to act autonomously in complex environments. 
To achieve this, an agent (a computational system interacting with its environment) must be able to know the consequences of its actions and construct a sequence of steps that transforms the current environment into a desired goal state. 
This process, known as Automated Planning, is crucial for applications ranging from logistics to autonomous robotics. 
A key strength of this approach is its domain independence: Generic solvers can be applied to diverse fields without modifying their core algorithms, making it highly flexible.

However, this generality comes at a computational cost. Planning problems are inherently difficult, often falling into NP-hard or PSPACE-complete complexity classes.
Consequently, Automated Planning was among the first substantial applications of Answer Set Programming (ASP) \parencite{Dimopoulos1997, LIFSCHITZ200239}. 

\section{Goal of the Thesis}
Despite the expressive power of ASP, a significant gap remains between modeling a problem and solving it effectively. 
Plans are often modeled sequentially by executing one action after another. 
In complex domains, this can lead to an explosion of the search-depth (planning horizon), rendering the search for a solution computationally infeasible.
In contrast, the field of Satisfiability Testing (SAT) has demonstrated that reasoning about actions can be made significantly more effective by exploiting parallelism \parencite{Kautz1996}. 
By allowing multiple non-conflicting actions to occur simultaneously, the necessary plan horizon can often be reduced significantly.
This thesis builds directly upon the framework proposed by \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}. 
It aims to revisit these parallel encoding strategies, investigating how they can be effectively applied within a  pipeline to overcome the limitations of sequential planning and enhance overall solver performance through optimizations.


\section{Structure of the Thesis}
Chapter \ref{theory} establishes the theoretical foundations. We formally define planning tasks and introduce the semantics for sequential and parallel plans. Subsequently, we provide a brief introduction to Answer Set Programming (ASP), present the specific encodings developed by \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, and provide formal proofs of their soundness.

Chapter \ref{method} describes the methodological approach and the implementation pipeline. Furthermore, we introduce different optimization strategies and describe the setup for the empirical evaluation.

In Chapter \ref{result}, we analyze the results of our experiments, discussing the trade-offs between the different encoding strategies.

Finally, we conclude the thesis in chapter \ref{conclusion} with a short summary and an outlook on future work.


\chapter{Theory}\label{theory}
\section{Introduction to Planning Task and Parallel Planning}


Before discussing the specific encodings and optimizations for parallel planning from \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, 
we formally define planning problems with multi-valued state variables. This formalism is based on the work of \textcite{Helmert_2006,DIMOPOULOS_2019} and serves as the theoretical foundation for the subsequent ASP encodings.

\begin{defi}[Multi-valued planning task]
    \label{def:MVPlan}
    A STRIPS-like multivalued planning task according to \textcite{Helmert_2006} is given by a 4 tuple $\langle \mathcal{F}, s_0, s_*, \mathcal{O} \rangle$, where:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\mathcal{F}$ is a finite set of state variables, also called \textbf{fluents}. Each $x \in \mathcal{F}$ is associated with a finite domain $\dom(x)$. In any given state, a fluent $x$ is assigned exactly one value $v \in \dom(x)$.
      \item $s_0$, the \textbf{initial state}, is a total function, such that $s_0(x) \in \dom(x)$ for each $x \in \mathcal{F}$. 
      %i.e. a function that maps each fluent to a value from its domain.
      \item $s_*$, the \textbf{goal conditions}, is a partial function, such that $s_*(x) \in \dom(x)$, for each $x \in \dom(s_*)$.
      % i.e. a function that specifies the desired values for a subset of fluents.
      \item $\mathcal{O}$ is a finite set of operators, also called \textbf{actions}. An action $a \in \mathcal O$ is a tuple $\langle a^c, a^e \rangle$, where $a^c$ is the \textit{precondition} and $a^e$ the \textit{postcondition} of $a$. 
      Both $a^c$ and $a^e$ are partial functions, such that $a^c(x) \in \dom(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(a^c)$ and $a^e(x) \in \dom(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(a^e)$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{defi}

\begin{remark}
  In the definition shown in \textcite{Helmert_2006}, the postconditon of actions, called \textbf{conditional effect}, is a triplet $\langle x, v, cond \rangle$. The condition \textit{cond} is a partial function, such that $cond(x) \in \dom(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(cond)$. If the condition holds in the current state, the fluent $x$ is assigned value $v$. 
  Note that the postcondition $a^e$ in our definition can be seen as a special case of the conditional effect, where the condition is empty.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
  Let us illustrate the definitions with a simple example. The goal is to leave the house by opening the door, going outside, and finally closing the door.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{F} = \{door, location\}$ such that $\dom(door) = \{open, closed\}$ and \\ $\quad \quad$ \mbox{$\dom(location) = \{inside, outside\}$}
    \item $s_0 = \{door{=}closed, location{=}inside\}$
    \item $s_* = \{door{=}closed, location{=}outside\}$
    \item $\mathcal{O} = \{open, close, leave\}$, where
    \vspace{-1em}
    \begin{align*}
      open &= \langle\{door{=}closed\}, \{door{=}open\}\rangle\\
      close &= \langle\{door{=}open\}, \{door{=}closed\}\rangle\\
      leave &= \langle\{location{=}inside, door{=}open\}, \{location{=}outside\}\rangle
    \end{align*}
  \end{itemize}
\end{example}

Intuitively, fluents can be understood as the dynamic properties that describe the configuration of the world (environment). In our example, this world is defined by the \textit{location} of the actor and the status of the \textit{door}.
Actions define the possible changes to the world, where the precondition establishes the necessary requirements for the action to be applicable and the postcondition specifies the resulting effect.
The goal conditions represent the desired outcome of the planning task, which is typically partial, meaning we only specify the values that are explicitly relevant, leaving other variables undefined.
The initial state represents the complete snapshot of the world at the start. In general, a state defines the environment at a specific point in time. Currently, our description of the world remains static, being restricted to this single initial state. To introduce dynamics, a transition mechanism is needed, which specifies how actions transform one state into another.

\begin{defi}[Successor state]
  Let $s$ be a state and $a \in \mathcal{O}$ an action.\\
  The \textit{successor state} $o(a,s)$, obtained by applying action $a$ in state $s$, is defined if the precondition of $a$ holds, i.e., $a^c(x) = s(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(a^c)$. Then
  \[
  s'(x) = o(a,s)(x) = \begin{cases} a^e(x) & \text{if } x \in \dom(a^e) \\ s(x) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \quad \text{for each } x \in \mathcal{F}.
  \]
\end{defi}
\begin{remark}
  Let $A_n = \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$ be a sequence of actions with length n. We define the application of $A_n$ to a state $s$ recursively by
  \[
    o(A_n,s) = o(a_n,o(A_{n-1},s)) \text{, where }A_{n-1} = \langle a_1, \dots, a_{n-1} \rangle,
  \]
  if $o(A_i, s)$ is defined for all $1 \le i \le n$. We additionally define $o(\emptyset, s) := s$ for the empty sequence of actions.
\end{remark}

Therefore, a (reachable) state is defined as one that can be obtained from the initial state $s_0$ by applying a sequence of actions.
All reachable states and the transitions between them can be represented as a directed graph. Each node corresponds to a distinct reachable state, while the edges represent the possible actions that can be applied to transition from one state to another.
With these fundamental building blocks, the necessary framework to describe a solution for a planning task is established.

\begin{defi}[Sequential plan]
  A \textit{sequential plan} is a sequence of actions $A_n = \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$, such that 
  $s' = o(A_n,s_0)$ is defined and $s'(x) = s_*(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(s_*)$.
\end{defi}

\begin{example}
  Consider the planning task from the previous example. One possible solution is $A = \langle open, leave, close \rangle$. Since
  \begin{align*}
    o(A, s_0) &= o(\langle open, leave, close \rangle, \{door{=}closed, location{=}inside\})\\
    &= o(close, o(\langle open, leave \rangle, \{door{=}closed, location{=}inside\}))\\
    &= o(close, o(leave, o(open, \{door{=}closed, location{=}inside\})))\\
    &= o(close, o(leave, \{door{=}open, location{=}inside\}))\\
    &= o(close, \{door{=}open, location{=}outside\})\\
    &= \{door{=}closed, location{=}outside\} \\ 
    &= s_*.
  \end{align*}
\end{example}
\hspace{-1.5em}

Moreover, a solution to a planning task is equivalent to finding a path from the source node $s_0$ to a target node that satisfies the goal condition $s_*$.
While the sequential approach is rather intuitive, searching through the entire state space is often inefficient, as the graph grows exponentially with the number of actions.
A possible way to reduce the horizon (i.e., the number of time steps needed to satisfy the goal condition) is to allow multiple actions to be executed simultaneously.
Since conflict may arise if actions interfere with each other, arbitrary sets of actions cannot simply be applied at once.
Consequently, a fundamental requirement for a set of actions is that they must \enquote{agree} on their postcondition. This is necessary, since if two actions attempt to assign different values to the same variable and a fluent can only take one value at a time, the resulting state would be undefined.
This ambiguity is resolved by introducing the concept of \textit{confluence}.

\begin{defi}[confluent]
  Let $A = \{a_1, \dots, a_n\} \subseteq \mathcal{O}$ be a set of actions.\\
  $A$ is \textit{confluent} $\logeq a_i^e(x) = a_j^e(x)$ for all $1 \le i < j \le n$ and each $x \in \dom(a_i^e) \cap \dom(a_j^e)$.
\end{defi}

\begin{remark}
  $A$ is confluent $\Rightarrow B$ is confluent for each $B \subseteq A$.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
  In our initial example, the set $\{open, leave\}$ is confluent because the domains of their effects are disjoint. Similarly, $\{close, leave\}$ is confluent.
  
  To illustrate more complex scenarios, we extend our example with two fluents, $light$ and $key$, where $\dom(light) = \dom(key) = \{0, 1\}$, and two corresponding actions:
  \begin{align*}
    \textit{turnOff} &= \langle \{location{=}inside, light{=}1\}, \{light{=}0\} \rangle \\
    \textit{getKey} &= \langle \{location{=}inside, key{=}0\}, \{key{=}1\} \rangle.
  \end{align*}
  Moreover, we modify the precondition of $open$ to require the key ($open^c{=}\{door{=}closed, key{=}1\}$) and update the goal condition to $s_* = \{location{=}outside, door{=}closed, light{=}0\}$.
  
  In this extended setting, the set $\{\mathit{getKey, open, leave, turnOff}\}$ is also confluent, since they target different variables ($key, door, location, light$). Thus, no conflicts in their effects occur.
\end{example}

The concept of confluence guarantees that the simultaneous execution of actions does not lead to a contradictory state.
However, ensuring consistent effects is only one part of the problem, as the preconditions of the actions must also be considered. Unlike in sequential planning, where preconditions are simply checked in the current state, parallel planning allows for different interpretations of precondition dependencies.
These interpretations lead to the different parallel representations, which have been investigated in the literature \parencite{Dimopoulos1997, RINTANEN20061031, Wehrle2007} and are specifically implemented and tested using Answer Set Programming in \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}.

\begin{defi}[Parallel representations]
  Let $s$ be a state and $A = \{a_1, \dots, a_n\}$ a confluent set of actions. Then $A$ is called:

  \begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1em}

    \item \makebox[4.8cm][l]{\textit{$\forall$-step serializable} in $s$} $\quad\logeq\quad$ 
    \parbox[t]{0.55\textwidth}{
        $o(\langle a_{\pi(1)}, \dots, a_{\pi(n)} \rangle, s)$ is defined \\ 
        for \textbf{all} permutations $\pi$.
    }

    \item \makebox[4.8cm][l]{\textit{$\exists$-step serializable} in $s$} $\quad\logeq\quad$ 
    \parbox[t]{0.55\textwidth}{
        $o(\langle a_{\pi(1)}, \dots, a_{\pi(n)} \rangle, s)$ is defined \\
        for \textbf{some} permutation $\pi$ and\\
        $a^c(x) = s(x)$ for each $a \in A$, $x \in \dom(a^c)$.
    }

    \item \textit{relaxed $\exists$-step serializable} in $s$ $\quad\logeq\quad$ 
    \parbox[t]{0.55\textwidth}{
        $o(\langle a_{\pi(1)}, \dots, a_{\pi(n)} \rangle, s)$ is defined \\
        for \textbf{some} permutation $\pi$.
    }
  \end{itemize}
\end{defi}

\begin{remark}
  $A$ is $\forall$-step serializable $\Rightarrow$ $A$ is $\exists$-step serializable $\Rightarrow$ $A$ is relaxed $\exists$-step serializable.
\end{remark}

As presented in the remark, these parallel representations form a hierarchy of restrictiveness regarding how the preconditions of actions in a set $A$ are satisfied.
The most restrictive representation is the $\forall$-step serialization, which requires strong independence among the actions. Since the set must be executable in \textit{any} order, no action in the set is permitted to invalidate the preconditions of another and the precondition of all actions in $A$ must hold in the current state $s$.
In contrast, the $\exists$-step serialization relaxes the independence requirement. While preconditions must still be satisfied in $s$ (i.e., actions in $A$ cannot \enquote{enable} each other), conflicts are allowed: One action $a \in A$ may invalidate the precondition of another action $a' \in A$, as long as there exists at least \textit{one} valid execution sequence where the conflicting action $a$ occurs after the affected action $a'$.
Finally, the relaxed $\exists$-step serialization is the least restrictive approach. It abandons the requirement that all preconditions must initially hold in $s$. Instead, it allows that an action $a \in A$ can establish the preconditions required by another action $a' \in A$, as long as a valid ordering exists in which the enabling action precedes the dependent one.
Based on these serialization concepts, we can now define the corresponding parallel plans.

\begin{defi}[Parallel Plans]
  \label{def:PPlan}
  Let $\mathcal A_m = \langle A_1, \dots, A_m \rangle$ be a sequence of confluent sets of actions. $\mathcal A_m$ is a \textit{$\forall$-step, $\exists$-step} or \textit{relaxed $\exists$-step plan} iff
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $s_m(x) = s_*(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(s_*)$
    \item $A_i$ is $\forall$-step, $\exists$-step or relaxed $\exists$-step serializable in $s_{i-1}$ for $1 \le i \le m$, where \\
    \[
      s_i(x) = \begin{cases} 
         a^e(x) & \text{if } \exists a \in A_i \text{ such that } x \in \dom(a^e) \\ 
         s_{i-1}(x) & \text{otherwise} 
      \end{cases} 
      \quad  \text{for each } x \in \mathcal F.
    \]
  \end{itemize}
\end{defi}

\begin{example}
  In our modified example we have:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\forall$-step plan: $\langle\{getKey, \mathit{turnOff}\}, \{open\}, \{leave\}, \{close\}\rangle$
    \item $\exists$-step plan: $\langle\{getKey, \mathit{turnOff}\}, \{open\}, \{leave, close\}\rangle$
    \item relaxed $\exists$-step plan: $\langle\{getKey, \mathit{turnOff}, open, leave\}, \{close\}\rangle$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{example}

While the concept of a sequential plan is straightforward; we are effectively dealing with transitions of the form $s \xrightarrow{a} s'$. 
However in parallel planning, we generalize this transition to allow sets of actions, i.e., $s \xrightarrow{A} s'$, effectively merging multiple actions together into a single one.
Notice how in our example the constraints on parallelism relax with each definition. In the $\exists$-step plan, the actions \textit{leave} and \textit{close} can be placed in the same step because there exists a valid sequence (\textit{leave} $\to$ \textit{close}).
The relaxed $\exists$-step plan achieves the highest compression. Here, actions can \enquote{enable} each other within the same step: \textit{getKey} establishes the precondition for \textit{open}, which in turn enables \textit{leave}. This \enquote{chaining} of effects allows us to reduce the first three sets of the $\forall$-step plan into a single one.

\subsection*{Summary}

In this section, the formal framework for multi-valued planning tasks was established.
We defined the static components of the world (fluents and states), the dynamic transition logic (applying actions), and the conditions for executing actions simultaneously.
With these formal definitions in place, the next section will translate this theoretical framework into Answer Set Programming encodings.


\section{Introduction to ASP} \label{asp encoding}
The following section presents a brief overview of Answer Set Programming (ASP). The core concepts and definitions introduced here are largely based on the Potassco User Guide \parencite{potassco_guide}.

Answer Set Programming is a declarative problem-solving approach oriented towards difficult (primarily NP-hard) search problems. Unlike imperative programming, where the programmer specifies the control flow to compute a solution, ASP follows a declarative paradigm: The problem is described as a logic program such that its valid models, called \textit{answer sets}, correspond to the solutions of the problem. This methodology allows for a clean separation between the problem description (the logic program) and the solving algorithms.

The theoretical foundation of ASP lies in the stable model semantics of logic programming \parencite{gel88}. To formally define these programs, we first introduce their basic building blocks.
The most fundamental units are \textit{atoms} of the form $p(t_1, \dots, t_n)$, where $p$ is a predicate symbol and $t_1, \dots, t_n$ with $n \in \mathbb {N}_0$ are terms.
Intuitively, an atom asserts that a specific relation $p$ holds for the tuple of arguments $(t_1, \dots, t_n)$. Therefore, it can either be true or false. 
These atoms are combined to form \textit{literals}. A literal is defined as either an atom $A$ or its default negation $\textit{not } A$. Here, $\textit{not}$ denotes negation as failure, meaning that $\textit{not } A$ is considered true if no proof for $A$ can be derived from the program.

\subsection*{Grounding and Solvers}
Atoms often contain variables (e.g., $p(X)$ instead of $p(1)$) to allow for compact problem encodings. However, standard ASP solvers operate on propositional logic and cannot handle variables directly. To bridge this gap, a process called \textit{grounding} is performed before the actual search. During grounding, the symbolic program is transformed into a variable-free program by replacing all variables with every possible combination of domain constants.
Consequently, modern ASP systems like \textit{clingo} \footnote{\url{https://potassco.org/clingo/}} consist of two main components: The grounder \textit{gringo}, which handles this transformation, and the solver \textit{clasp}, which performs the search for answer sets on the grounded program.

\subsection*{Logic Programs}
Based on these definitions, we can specify the structure of the programs used to model planning tasks. A \textit{normal logic program} is a finite set of facts, rules and integrity constraints of the form:
\[
    A_0 :\text{- } L_1, \dots, L_n.
\]
The \textit{head} of the rule is the atom $A_0$, and the \textit{body} is a conjunction of literals $L_1, \dots, L_n$, where $n \in \mathbb N_0$. The symbol \enquote{:-} (meaning $\leftarrow$) denotes logical implication: If all literals in the body are true, then the head will be true.
We distinguish three specific types of rules based on this structure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Facts} ($n=0$): Rules with an empty body, implying that the head atom $A_0$ is unconditionally true.
    \item \textbf{Rules} ($n \ge 1$): Rules that derive the head $A_0$ only if the body is satisfied.
    \item \textbf{Integrity Constraints} ($A_0$ is empty): Rules with an empty head, serving as a filter for solution candidates. If the body is satisfied, the rule yields a contradiction. Thus, at least one literal in the body must be false.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Planning Encoding}
A set of atoms is called a \textit{model} of a logic program if it satisfies all rules, facts, and integrity constraints. In ASP, a model is specifically called an \textit{answer set} (or stable model) if every atom in the model has a non-cyclic derivation.

We can translate the formal definition of a planning task into such an ASP logic program. Following the example in \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, the problem instance is represented purely by facts:
Fluents are defined via \texttt{fluent/1} and their domain via \texttt{value/2}, the initial state $s_0$ is defined via the predicate \texttt{init/2}, the goal $s_*$ via \texttt{goal/2}, and the pre- and postconditions of actions via \texttt{prec/3} and \texttt{post/3} respectively.

All planning algorithms presented in this work share a common incremental encoding (Listing \ref{core}) using the \textit{incmode} feature of \textit{clingo} \parencite{clingo14}.
The program is divided into three subprograms: \textit{base}, \textit{step(t)} and \textit{check(t)}.

The \textit{base} subprogram (Line 2) defines the initial state $s_0$, grounding the fluent values at timestep $0$. The predicate \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} is used to represent fluents $X \in \mathcal{F}$ that have value $V\in \dom(X)$ at timestep $t$.

The \textit{check(t)} subprogram (Line 4) is parametrized and starts at $t=0$. The integrity constraint enforces the goal condition, i.e. $s_*(X)=s_t(X)$ for each $X\in \dom(s_*)$. The external atom $query(t)$ ensures that this check is only active for the current search horizon $t$ (i.e., the current candidate plan length).

The \textit{step(t)} subprogram (Line 6--9) is grounded for each timestep $t$ starting at $t=1$. 
The choice rule in Line 6 creates the state candidates for timestep $t$, assigning every fluent $X$ exactly one value $V$ from its domain and the choice rule in Line 7 generates arbitrary sets of actions $A \in \mathcal O$ for the current timestep $t$ in the form of \texttt{occurs(A,t)}. These actions and the generated state must obey the integrity constraints in Lines 8 and 9.
Line 8 ensures that the postconditions of an occurring action is actually applied in the state. This also asserts the confluence of the set of actions: If the postconditions of two actions $a$ and $a'$ have different values for the same fluent (i.e. $V = a^e(X) \ne a'^e(X) = V'$), the uniqueness of the value in the choice rule (Line 6) would be violated.
Finally, Line 9 guarantees that fluents not affected by any action keep their value from the previous timestep. Any change in a fluent's value that is not explained by an action is rejected.

In summary, Listing \ref{core} establishes the state transitions with the postcondition of the chosen actions. 
However, it does not yet verify the \textit{applicability} of these actions regarding their preconditions, nor does it enforce the specific serializability requirements of the parallel semantics.

\subsection{Extension for \texorpdfstring{$\boldsymbol{\forall}$}{forall}-step plans}

The extension for $\forall$-step plans is shown in Listing \ref{forall}.

\begin{listing}[H]
  \caption{Extension of Listing \ref{core} for Encoding $\forall$-step Plans}
  \inputminted[frame=single,framesep=2mm,baselinestretch=1.2,linenos, firstline=2, firstnumber=1]{prolog}{encodings/EX_forall.lp}
  \label{forall}
\end{listing}

Line 1 is identical to the sequential encoding, ensuring that the preconditions of all chosen actions are satisfied in the previous state $s_{t-1}$.
To create a valid $\forall$-step plan, we must ensure that each set of actions is executable in \textit{any} order. 
Since Line 8 of the common core (Listing \ref{core}) already enforces confluence, the remaining requirement is that no action is allowed to invalidate the preconditions of another action.
We distinguish two types of interactions between an action $a$ and a fluent $X$ in its precondition:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Action $a$ has $X$ in its preconditions but does not change it, (i.e. $X \in \dom(a^c) \cap \dom(a^e)$ and $a^c(X) = a^e(X)$ or $X \in \dom(a^c)$ and $X \notin \dom(a^e)$)
  \item Action $a$ has $X$ in its preconditions and also changes its value (i.e. $X \in \dom(a^e)$ and $a^c(X) \ne a^e(X)$).
\end{enumerate}
Line 2 handles the first case by ensuring that $X$ has to remained unchanged (\enquote{\_} is a wildcard for any value).
However, the second case requires strict isolation of these kinds of actions. 
If an action $a$ invalidates its own precondition on fluent $X$, no other action $a'$ in the same timestep is allowed to modify $X$. 
If such an action $a'$ existed, the execution order $\langle a', a \rangle$ would fail because $a'$ would change $X$, invalidating the precondition of $a$.
To enforce this, Line 3 marks such actions using the predicate \texttt{single/2}. 
The integrity constraint in Line 4 then ensures that for any fluent $X$, at most one such action occurs per time step, preventing any interference.

\textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} present two different approaches to encode the extension for $\exists$-step plans, shown in Listings \ref{exists} and \ref{exists_alt}.
As before, Line 1 in both listings enforces the standard precondition check.
The difference lies in how they enforce the serializability requirement.

Listing \ref{exists_alt} implements a graph-based approach.
Since the common core (Listing \ref{core}) already establishes confluence, the only remaining requirement is the existence of a valid execution order. This effectively means excluding circular interference among the chosen actions.

This lends itself to an implementation using the \texttt{\#edge} directive \parencite{gebser_et_al:OASIcs.ICLP.2016.2} of \textit{clingo}, where built-in acyclicity checking \parencite{bomanson2016} is utilized.

The underlying structure corresponds to the \textit{conflict graph}. Following the definition in \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, we define the graph over all possible actions, with edges originating from the currently active set.
\begin{defi}[Conflict Graph]
  Let $A_t$ be the set of actions occurring at time $t$. The conflict graph $G_t = (\mathcal{O}, E)$ for timestep $t$ is a directed graph where
  \[
    E = \{\langle a, a' \rangle \mid \{a, a'\} \in A_t \times \mathcal{O} \setminus \{a\}, \exists X \in \dom(a^e) \cap \dom(a'^c): a^e(X) \ne a'^c(X)\}.
  \]
\end{defi}
Intuitively, there is a directed edge from $a$ to $a'$, if action $a$ invalidates a precondition of action $a'$.
Line 2 of Listing \ref{exists_alt} directly translates these edges into the \texttt{\#edge} syntax. 
A valid serialization of the actions corresponds to a reversed topological ordering of the subgraph induced by $A_t$.
Consequently, any answer set containing a cycle within the set of occurring actions is automatically rejected by the solver's cycle-checking logic.

Listing \ref{exists} implements this acyclicity checking by a recursive derivation of the predicates \texttt{apply/2} and \texttt{ready/2}. 
Line 4 defines the base case: Any action that does not occur is considered $ready$ (since it does not interfere with the execution order). Line 5 marks occuring actions: If an action is successfully applied, it is also considered $ready$.
Line 2--3 implement the ordering logic. An action $a$ can only be applied (\texttt{apply}) if all other actions $a'$ whose preconditions it invalidates are already applied or do not occur (\texttt{ready}). 
Line 6 is the final constraint: It requires that every occurring action must eventually become $ready$.
If the subgraph induced by $A_t$ contains a cycle (e.g., $a$ invalidates $a'$ and $a'$ invalidates $a$), neither \texttt{apply(a,t)} nor \texttt{apply(a',t)} can ever be derived, since each would wait for the other to be ready first. Consequently, the constraint in Line 6 would be violated, causing the solver to reject the model.

Finally, we present the extension from \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} for relaxed $\exists$-step plans in Listing \ref{relaxed_exists}.

\begin{listing}[H]
  \caption{Extension of Listing \ref{core} for Encoding Relaxed $\exists$-step Plans}
  \inputminted[frame=single,framesep=2mm,baselinestretch=1.2,linenos, firstline=2, firstnumber=1]{prolog}{encodings/EX_relaxed.lp}
  \label{relaxed_exists}

Structurally, it shares the same derivation approach with the standard $\exists$-step encoding, but it relaxes the applicability check.
The other encodings required that preconditions strictly had to hold in the previous state $s_{t-1}$. 
Here, actions within the same parallel set can provide \enquote{internal support} for each other. 
This means an action is applicable if its preconditions are established either by the previous state or by another action occurring earlier in the valid execution sequence of the current step.

This logic is implemented by the predicate \texttt{reach/3}, which represents the values of fluents that are currently available. A value is reachable if it holds in the previous state (Line 1) or if it is obtained by an action that has already been applied in the current internal sequence (Line~2).
The rule in Line 3 and 4 now checks against the reach predicate instead of the static state. Therefore, an action can safely be applied if its preconditions are reachable.
As in the standard encoding for $\exists$-step plans, we must still check acyclicity. Consequently, the integrity constraint in Line 7 guarantees that no such circular dependencies exist, ensuring a valid serialization.

\section{Proof of Correctness}
Having presented the ASP encodings for both sequential and the different parallel semantics, we now present the proof from \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} that formally verify their correctness.
The goal is to establish a one-to-one correspondence between the stable models of our logic programs and the valid plans as defined in Definition \ref{def:PPlan}.
We adopt the notation from \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} and decompose the logic programs into their constituent parts: 

\begin{itemize}
  \item $I$ denotes the set of facts representing the specific planning task instance $\langle \mathcal{F}, s_0, s_*, \mathcal{O} \rangle$ (as in Definition \ref{def:MVPlan}).
  \item $B$ corresponds to the rule in the \texttt{base} subprogram (Listing \ref{core}, Line 2).
  \item $Q(t)$ represents the goal verification rule defined in the \texttt{check(t)} subprogram (Listing \ref{core}, Line 4).
  \item $S(t)$ denotes the transition rules defined in the \texttt{step(t)} subprogram (Listing \ref{core}, \mbox{Lines 6--9}).
  \item $S^p(t)$ is the specific extension for $S(t)$, where:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $p = s$ for the sequential extension (Listing \ref{seq}),
        \item $p = \forall$ for the $\forall$-step extension (Listing \ref{forall}),
        \item $p = \exists$ for the $\exists$-step extension (Listing \ref{exists}),
        \item $p = E$ for the alternative $\exists$-step extension (Listing \ref{exists_alt}),
        \item $p = R$ for the relaxed $\exists$-step extension (Listing \ref{relaxed_exists}).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Using these components, the complete logic program for a horizon $n$ is defined as:
\[
  P^p(n) := I \cup B \cup \bigcup_{t=1}^{n} \left( S(t) \cup S^p(t) \cup Q(t) \right) \cup \{ \texttt{query(n)}. \}.
\]


\begin{theo}[Correctness of sequential plans \parencite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}]
  \label{theo:1}
  Let $A_n := \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$, $a_t \in \mathcal{O}$ for each $1 \le t \le n$, be a sequence of actions. Then,
  \begin{align*}
      A_n \text{ is a sequential plan} \iff &P^s(n) \text{ has a stable model } M \text{ such that} \\
        &\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs(a,t)} \in M \} = \{\langle a_t, t \rangle \mid 1 \le t \le n \}.
  \end{align*}
\end{theo}


\begin{proof}
  We prove both directions separately.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item[($\Rightarrow$):] 
    Assume $A_n$ is a sequential plan. \\
    We construct a candidate set $M$ based on the plan execution and show it is indeed a stable model of $P^s(n)$.
    The rule in B ensures that $\texttt{holds(X,V,0)} = s_0(X)$ for each $X \in \mathcal F$. The choice rule (Line 6 of Listing \ref{core}) enforces that for any $1 \le t \le n$ and $X \in \mathcal F$, exactly one value $V$ is assigned. 
    Moreover, the integrity constraints (Lines 8 and 9) in $S(t)$ dictate the transition logic between states. 
    For any $1 \le t \le n$, the value $V$ of a fluent $X$ is determined by:
    \[
      \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \leftrightarrow \begin{cases} V = a_t^e(X) & \text{if } X \in \text{dom}(a_t^e) \\ V = s_{t-1}(X) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
    \]
    Therefore, the set of \texttt{holds}-atoms in any stable model must satisfy:
    \begin{equation}
      \{ \langle X, V, t \rangle \mid \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \in M \}  = \{ \langle X, V, t \rangle \mid X \in \mathcal{F}, V {=} o(A_t, s_0)(X), 0 \le t \le n \}.
      \label{eq:holds-equivalence}
    \end{equation}
    Since the action sequence is fixed by the plan assumption ($\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs(a,t)} \in M \} = \{\langle a_t, t \rangle \mid 1 \le t \le n \}$), the only candidate for a stable model $P^s(n)$ is:
    \begin{align*}
      M = I &\cup \{ \texttt{occurs($a_t$,t)} \mid 1 \le t \le n \} \\
            &\cup \{\texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \mid (X, V, t) \text{ satisfies } \eqref{eq:holds-equivalence}\}    \cup \{\texttt{query(n)}\}.
    \end{align*}
     By the definition of a sequential plan, $o(a_t, s_{t-1})$ is defined for all $1 \le t \le n$, and the goal condition $s_n(X) = s_*$ for each $X \in \dom(s_*)$ is met. Thus, the integrity constraint regarding preconditions (Line 1, Listing \ref{seq}) and the goal check (Line 4, Listing \ref{core}) are satisfied. Since $M$ is a set satisfying all rules and constraints, it is indeed a stable model of $P^s(n)$.

  \item[($\Leftarrow$):]
    Assume that $M$ is a stable model of $P^s(n)$ s.t. $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs(a,t)} \in M \} = \{\langle a_t, t \rangle \mid 1 \le t \le n \}$. \\
    We prove by induction over $t$, that the following equation holds for each $1 \le t \le n$:
    \begin{equation}
      \{\langle X, V, t \rangle \mid \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \in M \} = \{\langle X, V, t \rangle \mid X \in \mathcal F, V=o(A_t, s_0)(X), 0 \le t \le n\}.
      \label{eq:holds-equivalence2}
    \end{equation}
    \textit{Base case }$t=0$: \\
    The rule in B directly implies that $\{\langle X, V, \rangle \mid \texttt{holds(X, V, 0)} \in M \} = \{\langle X, V \rangle \mid \\X \in \mathcal F, V = s_0(X)\}$,
    satisfying the equation for $t=0$. \\
    \textit{Induction step }$t-1 \mapsto t$: \\
    Assume \eqref{eq:holds-equivalence2} holds for $t-1$, i.e., the state at $t-1$ corresponds to $s_{t-1} = o(A_{t-1}, s_0)$. The integrity constraint in Line 1 of Listing \ref{seq} in $S^s(t)$ ensures that the successor state $s_t = o(a_{t}, o(A_{t-1}, s_0))$ is defined. Moreover, the choice rule in Line 6 of Listing \ref{core}, together with the integrity constraints in $S(t)$ guarantee that 
    \[
    \{\langle X, V, t \rangle \mid \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \in M\} = \{\langle X, V, t \rangle \mid X \in \mathcal F, V = o(A_t, s_0)(X), 0 \le t \le n\}.
    \]
    Thus, \eqref{eq:holds-equivalence2} holds for each $1 \le t \le n$.
    Given \texttt{query(n)}, the integrity constraint in $Q(n)$ yields that $o(A_n, s_0) = s_*(X)$ for each $X \in \dom(s_*)$ and therefore $A_n$ is a sequential plan. 
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{theo}[Correctness of parallel plans \parencite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}]
  Let $\mathcal A = \langle A_1, \dots, A_m \rangle$ be a sequence of sets of actions, $A_t \subseteq \mathcal{O}$ for $1 \le t \le m$ and $p \in \{\forall, \exists, R\}$. Then,
  \begin{align*}
      \mathcal A \text{ is a $p$-step plan} \iff &P^p(m) \text{ has a stable model } M \text{ such that} \\
        &\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs}(a,t) \in M \} = \{\langle a, t \rangle \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \}.
  \end{align*}
\end{theo}

\begin{proof}
  We again prove both directions separately.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item[($\Rightarrow$):]
      Assume $\mathcal A = \langle A_1, \dots, A_m \rangle$ is a $\forall$-/$\exists$-/relaxed $\exists$-step plan. \\
      For each $1 \le t \le m$, let $s_t$ be the state defined by applying the set $A_t$ to $s_{t-1}$. Due to the confluence of $A_t$, $s_t$ is well-defined as
      \[
        s_t(X) = \begin{cases} 
          a^e(X) & \text{for each } a \in A_t \text{ where } X \in \dom(a^e) \\ 
          s_{t-1}(X) & \text{otherwise}
        \end{cases} \quad \text{for each } X \in \mathcal F.
      \]
      Analogous to the proof of Theorem \ref{theo:1}, consider any stable model $M$ of $P^p(m)$ such that the action occurrences match the plan, i.e. $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs(a,t)} \in M\} = \{\langle a,t \rangle \mid \text{\mbox{$a \in A_t$}}, \text{\mbox{$1 \le t \le m$}} \}$.
      The rule in $B$, together with the choice rule and integrity constraints in $S(t)$, dictates the values of all fluents based on these actions. Thus, the set of \texttt{holds}-atoms in $M$ is determined as:
      \[
        \{\langle X,V,t\rangle \mid \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \in M\} = \{\langle X,V,t\rangle \mid X \in \mathcal F, V = s_t(X), 0 \le t \le m\}.
      \]
      We now distinguish the different cases of parallel plans: \\


      \textbf{Case 1:} $\mathcal A$ is a $\forall$-step plan. \\
      For $1 \le t \le m$, the atom \texttt{single(X,t)} defined in $S^\forall(t)$ (Line 3 of Listing \ref{forall}) is derived iff there exists some action $a \in A_t$ that invalidates its own precondition, i.e., $X$~$\in$~$\dom(a^c)$~$\cap \dom(a^e)$ and $a^c(X) \ne a^e(X)$. 
      Hence, $P^\forall(m)$ cannot have any stable model $M$ apart from
      \begin{align*}
        M = I &\cup \{\texttt{occurs(a,t)} \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m\} \\
              &\cup \{\texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \mid X \in \mathcal F, V = s_t(X), 0 \le t \le m\} \\
              &\cup \{\texttt{single(X,t)} \mid a \in A_t, X \in \dom(a^c) \cap \dom(a^e), a^c(X) \ne a^e(X), 1 \le t \le m \} \\
              &\cup \{\texttt{query(m)}\}.
      \end{align*}
      such that $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs(a,t)} \in M\} = \{\langle a, t \rangle \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m\}$.
      
      It remains to show that $M$ satisfies the integrity constraints in $S^\forall(t)$.
      Since for any $1 \le t \le m$, the successor state $s_t= o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined for \textit{any} sequence $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ with $\{a_1, \dots, a_k\} = A_t$, no action can interfere with the precondition of another action.
      Consequently, if \texttt{single(X,t)} is derived (meaning an action invalidates its own precondition on $X$), no other action can modify $X$, as this would create a conflict in at least one permutation. Therefore, the integrity constraint restricting the count of actions affecting $X$ with \texttt{single(X,t)} is satisfied.
      Analogously, the constraint requiring preconditions to remain stable (Line 1 and 2 of Listing \ref{forall}) is satisfied since $s_{t-1}(X)=a^c(X)$ for each $a \in A_t$, $X \in dom(a^c)$ and $1\le t \le m$.
      One can check that the remaining rules and integrity constraints in $S(t)$ are satisfied as well, so $M$ is indeed a stable model of $P^\forall(m)$. \\

      \textbf{Case 2:} $\mathcal A$ is a $\exists$-step plan. \\
      We analyze the programs $P^\exists(m)$ and $P^E(m)$ separately, starting with the acyclicity-based program $P^E(m)$. \vspace{0.5em}

      \textit{Analysis of }$P^E(m)$: \\
      By definition of an $\exists$-step plan, for every timestep $1 \le t \le m$, there is a sequence of actions $\langle a_1, \dots a_k\rangle$ with $\{a_1, \dots, a_k\} = A_t$ such that $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle, s_{t-1}) = s_t$ is defined. A valid serialization requires that no action invalidates the precondition of a subsequent action.
      Formally, let $G_t = (A_t, E_t)$ be the conflict graph for timestep $t$, where an edge represents a invalidated precondition:
      \[
        E_t = \{ (a, a') \in A_t \times A_t \mid a \ne a', \exists X \in \dom(a^e) \cap \dom(a'^c) : a^e(X) \ne a'^c(X) \}.
      \]
      Since a valid serialization exists, $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ constitutes a reversed topological ordering of $G_t$. By definition, a directed graph allows for such an ordering iff it contains no cycle. Thus, $G_t$ must be acyclic.

      Now consider the candidate model $M$:
      \begin{align*}
        M = I &\cup \{\texttt{occurs(a,t)} \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \} \\
              &\cup \{\texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \mid X \in \mathcal F, V=s_t(X), 0 \le t \le m\} \cup \{\texttt{query(m)}\}.
      \end{align*}
      The \texttt{\#edge} directive in Listing \ref{exists_alt} (Lines 2--3) generates an edge between two occurring actions $(a,t)$ and $(a',t)$ exactly if the condition for $E_t$ is met. Consequently, the set of edges declared in the ASP model corresponds exactly to the union of all conflict graphs $\bigcup_{t=1}^m E_t$.
      Since each $G_t$ is acyclic and the graphs for different timesteps $1 \le t < t' \le m$ are vertex-disjoint ($(a,t), \ne (a, t')$), the overall graph induced by $M$ is acyclic.
      Therefore, the acyclicity constraint is satisfied and $M$ is a stable model of $P^E(m)$. \vspace{0.5em}

      \textit{Analysis of }$P^\exists(m)$: \\
      The rules defining \texttt{apply} and \texttt{ready} in $S^\exists(t)$ (Lines 2--5 in Listing \ref{exists}) effectively traverse the dependency graph $G_t$.
      Since $G_t$ is acyclic, there exists a reversed topological ordering of the actions $a \in A_t$. The derivation of atoms proceeds along this ordering: Actions with no dependencies (sinks in $G_t$) are derived as \texttt{apply} first, thereby becoming \texttt{ready}. This satisfies the conditions for their predecessors. 
      Since $G_t$ is finite and acyclic, this inductive process reaches a fixpoint where \texttt{apply(a,t)} and \texttt{ready(a,t)} hold for each $a \in \mathcal O$ and $1 \le t \le m$.
      Consequently, the integrity constraint requiring all actions to be ready (Line 6 in Listing \ref{exists}) is satisfied.
      Thus, $P^\exists(m)$ extends the base model $M$ to:
      \[
        M' = M \cup \{\texttt{apply}(a,t) \mid a \in \mathcal O, 1 \le t \le m\} \cup \{\texttt{ready}(a,t) \mid a \in \mathcal O, 1 \le t \le m \}
      \]
      such that $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs(a,t)} \in M'\} = \{\langle a, t \rangle \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \}$.
      As established previously, the state transition rules are satisfied by $M$ (and thus by $M'$). Since the additional constraints in $P^\exists(m)$ regarding circularity are also satisfied by the completeness of the \texttt{ready} derivation, $M'$ is indeed a stable model of $P^\exists(m)$. \\

      \textbf{Case 3:} $\mathcal A$ is a relaxed $\exists$-step plan. \\
      For each $1 \le t \le m$, by definition of a relaxed $\exists$-step plan, there exists a serialization $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ of $A_t$ such that $\{a_1, \dots, a_k\} = A_t$ and the sequential execution is valid. This means $o(a_i, o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_{i-1} \rangle, s_{t-1}))$ is defined for all $1 \le i \le k$.
      
      Consequently, the preconditions of any action $a_i$ are satisfied by the state resulting from the application of its predecessors in $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$.
      The rules in $S^R(t)$ (Listing \ref{relaxed_exists}) simulate this reachability:
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item The atom \texttt{reach(X,V,t)} is derived for all values $V$ that are either present in $s_{t-1}$ or produced by an applied action.
        \item The atom \texttt{apply(a,t)} is derived for any action $a \in \mathcal O$ whose preconditions are satisfied by the reachable values.
        \item The atom \texttt{ready(a, t)} is derived for all $a \in \mathcal O$ (either because they are applied or because they do not occur).
      \end{enumerate} 
      Since the serialization $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ is valid, the least fixpoint of these rules guarantees that \texttt{apply(a,t)} is derived for each $a \in A_t$.
      
      Thus, the stable model $M$ of $P^R(m)$ corresponds to
      \begin{align*}
        M = I &\cup \{\texttt{occurs(a,t)} \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \mid X \in \mathcal F, V=s_t(X), 0 \le t \le m \} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{reach(X,V,t)} \mid X \in \mathcal F, V \in \{s_{t-1}, s_t\}, 1 \le t \le m\} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{apply(a,t)} \mid a \in \mathcal O: a^c(X) \in \{s_{t-1}(X), s_t(X)\} \text{ for each } X \in \dom(a^c)\} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{ready(a,t)} \mid a \in \mathcal O, 1 \le t \le m\} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{query(m)}\}.
      \end{align*}
      Since $M$ contains \texttt{ready(a,t)} for all actions $a \in \mathcal O$, the integrity constraint in Line 7 of Listing \ref{relaxed_exists} is satisfied. Furthermore, all state transition constraints are met by the definition of $s_t$.
      Therefore, $M$ is a stable model of $P^R(m)$.
    \item[($\Leftarrow$):]
      Assume that $M$ is a stable model of $P^p(m)$ such that $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs(a,t)} \in M \} = \{\langle a, t \rangle \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \}$. \\
      For any $1 \le t \le m$, the choice rule in Line 6 of Listing \ref{core} ensures that the set of \texttt{holds}-atoms induces a state function $s_t$ defined by $s_t(X) = V \Leftrightarrow \texttt{holds}(X,V,t) \in M$.
      
      Moreover, the rules and integrity constraints in $S(t)$ dictate the state transition. Specifically, the integrity constraint in Line 8 of Listing \ref{core} ensures for each $a \in A_t$ and $X \in \dom(a^e)$ that $s_t(X) = a^e(X)$ holds. Since $M$ is a stable model, contradicting effects on the same variable are impossible (as they would violate the constraints), which implies that $A_t$ is confluent.
      For unaffected fluents, the integrity constraint in Line 9 of Listing \ref{core} yield $s_t(X) = s_{t-1}(X)$ for each $X \in \mathcal F \setminus \bigcup_{a \in A_t} \dom(a^e)$. 
      
      Finally, given the fact \texttt{query(m)}, the integrity constraint in $Q(m)$ ensures that \mbox{$s_m(X) = s_*(X)$} for each $X \in \dom(s_*)$, thus satisfying the goal condition.
      
      We now distinguish the different cases to show that $A_t$ is $\forall$-/$\exists$- or relaxed $\exists$-step serializable in $s_{t-1}$ based on the specific constraints of each encoding. \vspace{0.5em}

      \textbf{Case 1: } To show: $A_t$ is $\forall$-step serializable. \\
      Let $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ be an arbitrary sequence of actions such that $\{a_1, \dots, a_k\}=A_t$.
      We prove by induction that for all $1 \le i \le k$, the transition $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_i \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined.
      \textit{Base case:} $i=1$: 

      The integrity constraint checking preconditions (Line 1 of Listing \ref{forall}) guarantees that $a_1$ is applicable in $s_{t-1}$, i.e., $o(a_1, s_{t-1})$ is defined. 

      \textit{Inductive step} $i \mapsto i+1$: 

      Assume $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_i \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined. We must show that $a_{i+1}$ is applicable in the resulting intermediate state $s_{temp} = o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_i \rangle, s_{t-1})$.
      Consider any fluent $X$ in the precondition of $a_{i+1}$. 
      If $X \notin \bigcup_{1 \le j \le i} \dom(a_j^e)$, then no preceding action has modified $X$. Thus $s_{temp}(X) = s_{t-1}(X)$, and the precondition holds trivially because $a_{i+1}$ is applicable in $s_{t-1}$.
      Therefore, we focus on the critical case where $X \in \dom(a_{i+1}^c) \cap \bigcup_{1 \le j \le i} \dom(a_j^e)$.
      This implies that there exists a preceding action $a_j$ that has a postcondition on $X$. We distinguish two cases based on the effect of $a_{i+1}$ itself:
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item $X \notin \dom(a_{i+1}^e)$: \\
        The integrity constraint in Line 2 of Listing \ref{forall} requires that if $a_{i+1}$ has a precondition on $X$ but no postcondition, the value of $X$ cannot change in the final state $s_t$. Since $a_j$ modifies $X$, we have $s_t(X) = a_j^e(X)$. The constraint implies $s_{t-1}(X) = s_t(X)$, meaning $a_j$ effectively did not change the value relative to $s_{t-1}$. Thus, \mbox{$s_{temp}(X) = a_{i+1}^c(X)$}.
        \item $X \in \dom(a_{i+1}^e)$: \\
        The rule in Line 3 of Listing \ref{forall} ensures that if an action ($a_{i+1}$) has a precondition on $X$ and sets a conflicting postcondition ($a^c(X) \neq a^e(X)$), it is marked as \texttt{single}. However, since another action $a_j$ also acts on $X$ (as established by the intersection), the integrity constraint in Line 4 would be violated.
        Since $M$ is a stable model, this implies that $\texttt{single(X,t)} \notin M$ and \mbox{$s_t(X) = a_{i+1}^e(X) = a_{i+1}^c(X)=s_{t-1}(X)$} must be the case.
      \end{enumerate} 
      In both cases, $s_{temp}(X) = a_{i+1}^c(X)$, so $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_{i+1} \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined.
      Therefore, by induction, the entire sequence is valid, and $A_t$ is $\forall$-step serializable. \vspace{0.5em}

      \textbf{Case 2: } To show: $A_t$ is $\exists$-step serializable. \\
      The integrity constraint in Line 1 of Listing \ref{exists} guarantees that every action $a \in A_t$ is applicable in $s_{t-1}$, i.e., $o(a, s_{t-1})$ is defined.
      Furthermore, the integrity constraint in Line 6 ensures that $\texttt{ready(a,t)} \in M$ for each $a \in \mathcal O$ and the rules in Lines 4--5 guarantee that $\texttt{apply(a,t)} \in M$ for each $a \in A_t$.
      The rule defining \texttt{apply} (Lines 2--3) imposes a dependency: An action $a$ can only be applied if all actions $a'$ whose preconditions it invalidates are marked as \texttt{ready}.
      Since $M$ is a stable model, there exists a derivation order of the \texttt{apply} atoms that respects these dependencies. Let $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ be the sequence of actions in $A_t$ ordered by their derivation step.
      Consider any action $a_i$ in the sequence and a fluent $X \in dom(a_i^c)$.
      Suppose that a preceding action $a_j$ ($j < i$) invalidates this precondition, i.e., $a_j^e(X) \neq a_i^c(X)$.
      By the definition of \texttt{apply} in the Lines 2--3, $a_j$ would have to wait for $a_i$ to become \texttt{ready} before it could be applied. This would imply that $a_i$ is derived before $a_j$, contradicting $j < i$.
      Therefore, for each $j < i$, $a_j$ does not invalidate $a_i$. Thus, $s_{t-1}(X)$ is either preserved ($s_{t-1}(X)=s_t(X)$) or modified confluently ($a_j^e(X) = a_i^c(X)$), ensuring $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined. 
      
      Regarding the alternative encoding using the \texttt{\#edge} directive (Listing \ref{exists_alt}), the graph logic ensures serializability explicitly.
      For any $1 \le t \le m$, the directive defines the conflict graph $G_t = (A_t, E_t)$ where a directed edge $(a, a') \in E_t$ exists iff action $a$ invalidates a precondition of action $a'$. 
      Since $P^E(m)$ enforces that $G_t$ is acyclic, there exists a sequence $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k\rangle$ with $\{a_1,\dots,a_k\} = A_t$, such that no edge connects any predecessor to a successor. Formally, for any $a_i$ in the sequence, there is no $a_j$ with $j < i$ such that $(a_j, a_i) \in E_t$.
      This absence of destructive edges implies that for any $X$ in the precondition, either no predecessor changes $X$ or it is modified consistently, i.e. $s_{t-1}(X) = a_i^c(X)$ or $a_j^e(X) = a_i^c(X)$.
      Thus, $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_i \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined for each $1 \le i \le k$, and holds in particular for the complete sequence $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle, s_{t-1})$, verifying that $A_t$ is $\exists$-step serializable. \vspace{0.5em}

      \textbf{Case 3: } To show: $A_t$ is relaxed $\exists$-step serializable. \\
      In view of the rule in Line 1 of Listing \ref{relaxed_exists}, a fluent value is reachable if $s_{t-1}(X)=V$.
      The recursive structure of the rules for \texttt{apply} (Lines 3--4) and \texttt{reach} (Line 2) imposes a dependency ordering: An action $a$ is only derived as \texttt{apply(a,t)} if all its preconditions are already in the extension of the \texttt{reach} predicate.

      Since $M$ is a stable model, there exists a finite derivation sequence for all atoms in $M$. Let $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ be the sequence of actions in $A_t$ ordered by the step in which their corresponding \texttt{apply} atom was derived (or satisfied).
      We prove that this sequence is a valid serialization.

      Consider any action $a_i$ in this sequence and any fluent $X$ in the precondition.
      The derivation of \texttt{apply($a_i$,t)} requires that \texttt{reach($X, a_i^c(X), t$)} was previously derived. By the definition of the \texttt{reach} rules (Line 1--2), this value must originate either from:
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item the state $s_{t-1}$ (Line 1), which implies $s_{t-1}(X) = a_i^c(X)$.
        \item the effect of a previously applied action $a_j$ (Line 2), which implies $a_j \in \{a_1, \dots, a_{i-1}\}$ and $a_j^e(X) = a_i^c(X)$.
      \end{enumerate}
      In both cases, the precondition of $a_i$ is satisfied by the state constructed from $s_{t-1}$ and the effects of the predecessors $\langle a_1, \dots, a_{i-1} \rangle$.
      Thus, $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined and $A_t$ is relaxed $\exists$-step serializable.
  \end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\section{Pipeline Overview}

To systematically evaluate and compare the performance of the proposed parallel planning encodings against each other and the sequential baseline, we implemented an automated pipeline using the \textit{lab} framework \parencite{seipp-et-al-zenodo2017}.
The benchmark set\footnote{\url{https://github.com/aibasel/downward-benchmarks}} consists of an unofficial collection of IPC instances provided by the Fast Downward project.

As illustrated in Figure \ref{pipeline}, the workflow proceeds in four main stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Preprocessing:} Starting with a planning task defined in PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language), we use \textit{cpddl} \parencite{fiser_2025_16423302} to convert the problem into a Finite Domain Representation (FDR).
    This step also includes the generation of invariant constraints, such as mutex groups, which can be leveraged for optimization later.
    \item \textbf{Translation:} The \textit{plasp} tool translates this FDR specification into a set of ASP facts. A detailed description of this translation process can be found in \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}.
    \item \textbf{Solving:} In the core step, \textit{clingo} combines these facts with our modified incremental encodings (sequential, $\forall$-step, $\exists$-step, or \textit{relaxed} $\exists$-step). Afterwards, the solver searches for one stable model corresponding to a valid plan.
    \item \textbf{Validation:} Finally, to ensure correctness, the resulting ASP-based plan (represented by the \texttt{occurs/2} atoms) is converted back into a sequential plan format. We employ the post-processing encoding provided by \textit{plasp} for serialization and subsequently format the output to be readable by the plan validator VAL\footnote{\url{https://github.com/KCL-Planning/VAL}}.
\end{enumerate}

Each component of this pipeline involves specific configurations and optimizations, which will be explained in detail in the following sections.


\section{Encoding Adaptation}
To integrate the theoretical encodings from \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} presented in Section \ref{asp encoding} into the automated pipeline, small changes are required to bridge the gap between the \textit{plasp} output format and the internal predicates used by our logic programs.
The necessary adaptations are shown in Listing \ref{core_mod}.
\begin{listing}[H]
\caption{Modifications to the Core Encoding}
\label{core_mod}
\begin{minted}[frame=single,framesep=2mm,baselinestretch=1.2,linenos]{prolog}
#include <incmode>.
#program base.
fluent(X) :- variable(X).
fluent(X,V) :- initialState(X,V), fluent(X).
value(X,V) :- contains(X, V).
prec(A, X, V) :- precondition(A, X, V).
post(A, X, V) :- postcondition(A, _, X, V).
...
:- fluent(X), #count{V : holds(X,V,0)} > 1.
...
#program step(t).
...
#show.
#show occurs(A, t): occurs(A, t).
\end{minted}
\end{listing}

Lines 3--6 map the predicates generated by \textit{plasp} (e.g., \texttt{initialState}, \texttt{contains}) to the predicates used in our encodings.
Line 7 handles the action effects. 
By using the wildcard \enquote{\texttt{\_}} for the second argument, we discard the specific effect identifiers. This simplification is chosen to reduce the complexity for the solver, since explicitly tracking every effect could inflate the ground program and force \textit{clingo} to manage a significantly larger set of atoms. This could degrade performance without necessarily improving the quality of the resulting plans. Therefore, it is assumed that the effect conditions are implicitly satisfied. Thus, the predicate \texttt{post}  captures the potential effects of actions without encoding the conditions under which they occur.
Line 9 performs a sanity check to ensure that the initial state is consistent (each fluent has at most one value).
Finally, Lines 13--14 act as an output filter, ensuring that only the relevant \texttt{occurs/2} atoms are displayed in the final answer sets.

\subsection{Post-Processing}
The stable models computed by \textit{clingo} are represented as sets of atoms (e.g., \\ \texttt{occurs(action(\enquote{move},\enquote{a},\enquote{b}), 1)}).
Standard plan validators like \textit{VAL}, however, require plans in a specific text-based format (often referred to as SAS plan) structured as \mbox{\texttt{time:(action args)}}, where \texttt{time} denotes time step $t$, \texttt{action} the action name and \texttt{args} its arguments.
We therefore implement a lightweight post-processing module in Python.
Briefly, the script iterates through the output string to identify all \texttt{occurs} atoms using regular expressions. 
For each match, it extracts the time step $t$ and the action arguments, stripping internal quotes and formatting delimiters. 
Finally, the extracted pairs are written to a file in a line-by-line format compatible with the validator.
This conversion ensures that the parallel plans found by our encodings can be verified against the original PDDL domain and problem specification.

\section{Optimizations}
While the encoding described this far correctly models the planning semantics and produces valid plans, the search space for complex problems can grow too large to solve the problem in a feasible time. To mitigate this and enhance solver performance, we integrate several optimization aiming to prune the search space.

\subsection{Mutex Groups}
Our primary optimization relies on \textit{mutex groups} (mutually exclusive sets of facts) provided by the \textit{cpddl} preprocessor.
A set of facts $M \subseteq \mathcal F$ is called a \textit{mutex group} iff $| M \cap s | \le 1$ for all reachable states $s$.

Intuitively, a mutex group represents a set of mutually exclusive facts, meaning that at most one fact from the set can hold in any valid state. Enforcing these invariants as integrity constraints has the potential to significantly prune the search space by detecting invalid partial states early.
We extend our encoding with the rules shown in Listing \ref{mutex}.

\begin{listing}[H]
\caption{Expansion for Mutex Groups}
\begin{minted}[frame=single,framesep=2mm,baselinestretch=1.2,linenos]{prolog}
#program base.
...
mutex(G,X) :- mutexGroup(G), contains(G,X,V), fluent(X,V).
mutex(G)   :- mutexGroup(G), #count{X : mutex(G,X)} > 1.
:- mutex(G), #count{X,V : holds(X,V,0), contains(G,X,V)} > 1.
...
#program step(t).
...
:- mutex(G), #count{X,V : holds(X,V,t), contains(G,X,V)} > 1.
...
\end{minted}
\label{mutex}
\end{listing}

The mutex information is provided by \textit{plasp} via the predicates \texttt{mutexGroup/1} and \texttt{contains/3}.
In the base program, we first define which fluents belong to a mutex group (Line 3), where a group must contain at least two fluents (Line 4).
Both integrity constraints in Line 5 and 9 ensures that no more than one fluent of each mutex group holds in each state.

\subsection{Guess-and-Check}
A crucial optimization for the $\exists$-step plan encodings is the \textit{guess-and-check} approach \parencite{eiter2006}. 
The core idea is to decouple the search for action sets from the computationally expensive verification of acyclicity.
Instead of enforcing serializability directly during the search, we first \enquote{guess} a candidate solution that satisfies all local constraints (preconditions, effects, confluence) but ignores potential cycles. In a second step, we \enquote{check} this candidate for circular invalidations.

Formally, we decompose the problem into a pair of logic programs $\langle G, C \rangle$, where $G$ is the \textit{generator} or \textit{guesser} and $C$ is the \textit{checker}.
A stable model $M$ of $G$ represents a valid plan iff the checker program, when grounded with the actions from $M$, is \textbf{unsatisfiable} (i.e., no cycle can be derived).

To implement the generator $G$, \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} proposes using the core program ($S(t)$ from Listing \ref{core}) combined with the precondition checks (Line 1 of Listing \ref{seq}, \ref{forall}, and \ref{exists}) and the problem instance facts.
Consequently, a stable model $M$ of $G$ guarantees that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Preconditions hold at $t-1$ for all chosen actions.
    \item Postconditions are applied and confluent (via Line 8 of Listing \ref{core}).
\end{enumerate}
The only property remaining to be verified is the absence of circular invalidations (cycles in the conflict graph), where actions invalidates each other's preconditions (e.g., $a^e(X) \neq a'^c(X)$ and $a'^e(X') \neq a'^c(X')$).

This verification is performed by the program $C$, shown in Listing \ref{check}. 
The inputs to $C$ are the action occurrences (\texttt{occurs/2}) from the candidate model $M$, along with the problem instance facts.

\begin{listing}[H]
  \caption{Encoding for Detecting Circular Invalidation (Check Program)}
  \inputminted[frame=single,framesep=2mm,baselinestretch=1.2,linenos]{prolog}{encodings/EX_check.lp}
  \label{check}
\end{listing}
The check program works by attempting to construct a valid serialization order using the predicate \texttt{ready}. Like in the encoding of Listing \ref{exists}, Lines 7--11 try to derive \texttt{ready(A,t)} for all actions that can be applied safely.
Line 12 derives the atom \texttt{cycle(t)} if there exists any occurring action $A$ that never becomes \texttt{ready}. This indicates that $A$ is part of a invalidation cycle.
Crucially, the program is designed to be \textbf{satisfiable} only if a cycle exists (Line 3).

Thus, if $C \cup M$ is satisfiable, a circular invalidation exists at some step $t$, meaning no valid permutation of the actions $A_t$ exists. This implies the candidate is not serializable and must be rejected.
Conversely, if $C \cup M$ is unsatisfiable, no cycle was found, and the candidate $M$ yields a valid plan.

\subsection{Backward-Propagating Heuristic}
To further optimize the solving process, we employ domain-independent heuristics using the \texttt{\#heuristic} directive of \textit{clingo}.
Following the approach described by \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, we modify the solver's search strategy by assigning specific attributes to atoms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{level} ($\in \mathbb{Z}$, default $0$): Determines the decision priority. The solver selects atoms with higher levels before those with lower levels.
  \item \textit{sign} ($\in \mathbb{Z}$, default $0$): Determines the preferred truth value. A positive value ($>0$) biases the solver to assign \textit{true}, a negative value ($<0$) to assign \textit{false}. If the value is $0$, the solver falls back to its default \textit{sign} heuristic.
\end{itemize}

Listing \ref{heuristic} implements a \textit{backward propagating} heuristic.
The core idea is to leverage the goal condition and bias the search towards establishing the goal fluents as early as possible in the plan.
\begin{listing}[H]
  \caption{Backward Propagating Heuristic}
  \inputminted[frame=single,framesep=2mm,baselinestretch=1.2,linenos]{prolog}{encodings/heuristic.lp}
  \label{heuristic}
\end{listing}

The priority level $l$ in Listing \ref{heuristic} is $2^{31}-1-t$ (the largest integer supported by \textit{clingo}), assigning the highest priority to the earliest time steps (since smaller $t$ yields a larger $l$).
The logic in Lines 2 and 3 effectively projects state information backwards from $t$ to $t-1$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Positive Bias (Line 2): If \textit{clingo} determines that \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} is true, the heuristic activates and suggests setting \texttt{holds(X,V,t-1)} to true as well.
    \item Negative Bias (Line 3): Conversely, if \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} becomes false, the heuristic biases \texttt{holds(X,V,t-1)} to be false.
\end{itemize} 
Intuitively, this encourages the solver to treat goal fluents as persistent, assuming they already held in step $t-1$ unless an action forces a change.
This creates a \enquote{pull} effect from the goal backwards to the initial state $s_0$.


\section{Experimental Setup}
To evaluate the performance of the different planning encodings and the effectiveness of the proposed optimizations, we performed a systematic comparison across four distinct experimental configurations.

\subsection{Configurations and Encodings}
We test the sequential encoding alongside the $\forall$-step, both $\exists$-step and the relaxed $\exists$-step plan encodings. 
Additionally, we evaluated the \textit{guess-and-check} strategy with a fallback mechanism: If the guess-and-check approach fails to find a valid serializable plan (or a cycle is found), the system automatically falls back to another encoding (we use the encoding for $\forall$-step plans). In such cases, the reported runtime is the sum of the time spent on the failed check and the time required by the fallback strategy. If this cumulative time exceeds the time limit, the instance is considered unsolved.

To isolate the impact of our optimizations, we run the complete benchmark set in four configurations, varying the usage of mutexes and heuristics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline:} No extra mutex detection, default solver heuristics.
    \item \textbf{Mutex Groups:} \textit{cpddl} enabled with the $h^2$ option to compute additional mutex groups.
    \item \textbf{Heuristics:} Using the backward propagating heuristic (Listing \ref{heuristic}).
    \item \textbf{Combined:} Both $h^2$ algorithm and backward heuristics enabled.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Benchmark Selection and Validation Criteria}
\textbf{Validation Criteria:}
For a planning task to be considered \textit{solved}, finding a candidate solution via \textit{clingo} is necessary but not sufficient. 
We enforce a strict validation protocol: A run counts as solved if and only if:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The solver produces an answer set within a 30-minute time limit.
    \item The extracted plan is successfully validated by the external validator VAL.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Benchmark Domains:}
We evaluated our approach on a suite of benchmarks from the International Planning Competitions (IPC). 
The test set comprises domains used in previous competitions, specifically selected in their STRIPS (Stanford Research Institute Problem Solver) formulations to ensure compatibility with our encodings.
The diverse set allows us to analyze the performance of parallel encodings across different problem structures (e.g., transportation, construction).

\subsection{Computational Environment}
The experiments were executed on \textit{bwUniCluster 3.0} \footnote{\url{https://wiki.bwhpc.de/e/BwUniCluster3.0}} at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT). 
Computations were performed on standard compute nodes restricted to a single CPU core to ensure comparability.
We imposed a strict time limit of 30 minutes (1800 seconds) and a memory limit of 32 GiB for the execution of \textit{clingo} and \textit{cpddl} each.



\chapter{Results} \label{result}
\section{Overall Performance}

\subsection{Coverage Analysis}
The coverage results in Figure \ref{fig:solved} reveal several trends regarding the effectiveness of parallel planning.

\paragraph{Sequential vs. Parallel Performance:}
In domains that allow for a high degree of concurrency, parallel encodings significantly outperform the sequential approach.
Notable examples include \textit{airport}, \textit{woodworking}, and both \textit{logistics} variants. 
As an example, in \textit{woodworking}, the sequential encoding ($S$) solves only 7 out of 50 instances, whereas all parallel encodings (except for guess-and-check) solve the entire set.
Similarly, in \textit{floortile}, the sequential encoding fails to solve any instance (0/40), while the relaxed $\exists$-step plan encoding achieves a coverage of 38/40. 
This confirms that the reduced time horizon enabled by parallel semantics can effectively mitigate the search depth explosion inherent to sequential planning. This is on par with the experiment of \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, where sequential encoding falls behind the parallel variants.
However, in more complex or inherently sequential domains like \textit{agricola} or \textit{snake}, this performance advantage is not observed. 

\paragraph{Comparison of Parallel Encodings:}
Comparing the different parallel semantics reveals a trade-off between \textit{plan flexibility} (allowing shorter horizons) and \textit{encoding complexity} (grounding and solving overhead):

\textbf{$\forall$-step plan:} Although it enforces the strictest requirements for parallel execution, the encoding for $\forall$-step plans shows better performance in comparison to the other parallel encodings in several domains (e.g., \textit{tetris}, \textit{parcprinter}), often outperforming even the more flexible variants in domains like \textit{hiking} or \textit{scanalyzer}.
This suggests that for many IPC domains, the capability to interleave dependent actions is not critical. Consequently, the advantage of the $\forall$-step plan semantics lies in its compactness: It requires fewer constraints and auxiliary atoms than the $\exists$-step plan variants, thereby significantly reducing the burden on the grounder and solver. This is on par with the findings of \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, where $\forall$-step plans performs the best among the parallel encodings.

\textbf{$\exists$-step plan:} The strength of the $\exists$-step plan semantics is seen in domains with complex dependencies. A prime example is \textit{floortile}, where the strict $\forall$-step plan encoding solves only 18 out of 40 instances, whereas the $\exists$-step plan encoding achieves a coverage of 37/40. 
This demonstrates that the increased flexibility of the $\exists$-step plan semantics enables the discovery of plans in constrained environments where the $\forall$-step plan approach fails. 
Regarding the implementation variants, the graph-based alternative ($E$) generally performs on par with or marginally better than the standard constructive encoding ($\exists$) as in \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, showing no significant deviation in total coverage.

\textbf{Relaxed $\exists$-step plan:} The relaxed encoding typically yields lower coverage compared to the standard $\exists$-step plan variants, as observed in domains like \textit{quantum-layout}. Although the \textit{relaxed} $\exists$-step plan encoding theoretically enables the shortest plan horizons by allowing actions to support each other within the same step, the computational cost is significant.
The recursive definition of the reachability predicates (\texttt{reach/3}) increases the complexity of the ground program.
Consequently, the overhead of solving these dense constraints often outweighs the benefits gained from a possible reduced time horizon.

\textbf{Guess-and-Check strategy:} The performance of the guess-and-check strategy is generally inferior to the direct encodings ($\exists$ and $E$). 
This is primarily due to the high rate of validity check failures. When a guessed plan contains a cycle, the pipeline is forced to trigger a costly fallback mechanism.
In these cases, which account for approximately 60\% of all runs, the cumulative runtime (combining the failed guess with the fallback search) frequently leads to timeouts on instances that pure $\forall$-step or $\exists$-step plan encodings could solve within the time limit.
Consequently, this strategy proves less robust for general-purpose planning compared to the integrated approaches and even solves the fewest total instances compared to the other parallel encodings. 

\subsection{Runtime Analysis}

\subsubsection{Overview Across Domains}
As seen in Figure \ref{fig:time} The sequential encoding ($S$) generally exhibits significantly higher runtimes compared to the parallel approaches. This discrepancy is particularly pronounced in domains with high concurrency potential, such as \textit{Gripper}, where the average runtime for $S$ is approximately 430 seconds, whereas the $\forall$-step plan encoding requires less than 3 seconds. This confirms that the reduced plan horizon in parallel planning typically translates to reduced solving time.

Among the parallel variants, the $\forall$-step plan encoding consistently achieves the lowest runtimes, rarely exceeding 100 seconds even on complex instances. 
This performance advantage can be attributed to the compactness of the encoding, which imposes the least burden on the grounder and solver.

The $\exists$-step plan encodings ($\exists$ and $E$) display higher variability. While they allow for shorter plan lengths, the increased complexity of the compatibility checks often leads to longer solving times compared to $\forall$-step plan, particularly in dependency-heavy domains like \textit{freecell}.

The direct comparison between the two main parallel semantics highlights the trade-off mentioned above.

\section{Impact of Optimizations}
To evaluate the effectiveness of the implemented optimizations, we compared the baseline encoding against configurations equipped with $h^2$ search for mutexes, the backward-propagating heuristic, and a combination of both. Figure \ref{fig:scatter_matrix_opti} provides a detailed runtime comparison of these configurations. Each point represents an instance solved by both encodings, with the x-coordinate indicating the runtime of the first encoding and the y-coordinate that of the second. Points below the diagonal indicate better performance of the first encoding.

\paragraph{Heuristic Guidance (Fig. \ref{fig:base_vs_h}):}
Contrary to expectations, the backward-propagating heuristic did not yield a significant improvement in coverage or runtime. 
As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:base_vs_h}, the data points align almost perfectly with the diagonal, indicating negligible deviation from the baseline performance. 
This suggests that the search from the goal backwards do not substantially reduce the search time compared to \textit{clingo}'s internal heuristic. This is different from the findings of \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, where the heuristic showed a noticeable improvement in some domains. It is also worth noting that the effectiveness of such heuristics is often highly domain-dependent. While \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} observed improvements, our evaluation covers a broader set of IPC instances where the goal distance might be less informative. Additionally, advancements in the underlying ASP solver since 2019 may have reduced the margin for improvement that external heuristics can provide.

\paragraph{Impact of More Mutex Groups (Fig. \ref{fig:base_vs_s_m_clingo} \& \ref{fig:base_vs_s_m_total}):}
In contrast, the inclusion of the $h^2$-algorithm for \textit{cpddl} resulted in a measurable performance gain. The impact is particularly emphasized in highly constrained domains such as \textit{Agricola}. In this domain, the sequential encoding solves 7 instances (compared to only 1 in the baseline) and the $\forall$-step plan encoding improves from 0 to 5 solved instances.

Analyzing the runtime reveals a trade-off between solving power and preprocessing overhead:

\textbf{Solver Efficiency (Fig. \ref{fig:base_vs_s_m_clingo}):} When looking exclusively at the solver runtime (\textit{clingo} time), the additional mutex groups provide a drastic speedup. Most points lie well below the diagonal, showing that the mutex constraints effectively prune the search space.

\textbf{Total Runtime (Fig. \ref{fig:base_vs_s_m_total}):} When accounting for the full pipeline duration (including \textit{cpddl} preprocessing), a trade-off becomes apparent. 
For many instances, the overall runtime does not significantly improve. In fact, for easier instances, the overhead of longer searching for mutex groups outweighs the time saved during the plan search, leading to a longer total runtime compared to the baseline.
This effect is particularly seen in the $\forall$-step plan encoding, which is rather compact and thus benefits less from additional pruning.
In comparison, for the relaxed $\exists$-step plan encoding, the total runtime with more mutex groups is often lower than the baseline, indicating that ''heavier'' encodings profit more from the invariants.

\section{Conclusion of Results}
The experimental evaluation leads to several key conclusions regarding the performance and characteristics of ASP-based planning encodings.
First and foremost, the results demonstrate that parallelism is the primary driver for efficiency in ASP planning. Parallel encodings significantly outperform the sequential baseline across almost all standard benchmarks, primarily because they drastically reduce the required planning horizon. This reduction effectively mitigates the search depth explosion that renders sequential approaches infeasible for larger instances.

Regarding the comparison of parallel semantics, it became evident that compactness often outweighs theoretical flexibility. The encoding for $\forall$-step plans proved to be the most robust variant. Although the $\exists$-step plan semantics theoretically allow for a higher compression of plans by merging more actions per step, the associated overhead of grounding the complex compatibility constraints often negates this advantage. However, in scenarios where the $\exists$-step plan semantics is explicitly required to find a solution, the graph-based implementation ($E$) utilizing the \texttt{\#edge} directive showed superior performance compared to the constructive approach.

Furthermore, the analysis highlights the critical importance of preprocessing. The integration of invariants, specifically mutex group constraints derived from the $h^2$ algorithm, proved essential for solving highly constrained instances. In contrast, simple domain-independent heuristics, such as backward propagation, failed to provide a tangible benefit over the solver's built-in strategies, suggesting that effective guidance in ASP requires more domain-specific control knowledge.

Moreover, a comparison of plan lengths reveals a crucial trade-off. While parallel encodings find solutions faster, the resulting sequentialized plans are often longer than those produced by the sequential encoding. This indicates that a minimal parallel plan does not necessarily correspond to minimal actions. From a practical perspective, this is a critical consideration, since in real-world applications where actions correspond to time and financial costs, the runtime advantage of parallel encodings must be balanced against the potentially lower quality of the resulting plans.

The results are mostly consistent with the findings of \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, with a notable exception regarding the impact of heuristic guidance and the guess-and-check strategy. While their study identified the combination of domain-independent heuristics and guess-and-check as the superior approach, our evaluation did not replicate this benefit. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the underlying solving control: whereas \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} implemented custom algorithms for iterative plan extension, our approach relies on \textit{clingo}'s \texttt{incmode}. It appears that the internal optimizations and conflict-driven heuristics of the incremental solver are sufficiently robust to overshadow the static guidance provided by the backward-propagating heuristic, rendering the additional overhead of the guess-and-check decomposition counterproductive in our setup.

In summary, our findings suggest that while advanced ASP constructs like external heuristics and complex step semantics offer theoretical advantages, the most effective strategy for standard PDDL benchmarks often lies in efficient, compact encodings combined with strong preprocessing.


\chapter{Conclusion} \label{conclusion}

In this thesis, we revisited the parallel planning framework introduced by \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, providing a re-evaluation of their encodings.

The central focus of this work was the formalization and implementation of parallel planning semantics. We investigated distinct approaches for executing actions simultaneously, ranging from the strict $\forall$-step plan semantics to the more flexible (relaxed) $\exists$-step plan semantics. 
We analyzed the theoretical properties of these semantics, detailed their ASP encodings, and reproduced the proof of their soundness as established by \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}

Beyond the theoretical foundations, we implemented a robust pipeline to evaluate the parallel plans on a collection of IPC benchmarks and integrated different optimizations to prune the search space, most notably mutex groups derived from the $h^2$-algorithm.
We also explored the use of backward-propagating heuristics described in \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} and tested a guess-and-check strategy for the (relaxed) $exists$-step plan semantics.

The experimental evaluation showed the practical viability of this approach. 
Our results demonstrated that parallel encodings significantly outperform the sequential baseline by reducing the planning horizon. Additionally, the inclusion of precomputed mutex groups generally improves the performance while neither the backward-propagating heuristic nor the guess-and-check strategy provided a significant advantage over the baseline. 

While this work redemonstrates the potential of ASP for planning tasks, there are several improvements and adaptations for future research.
One promising direction is the investigation of alternative strategies for determining the planning horizon. Instead of relying solely on \textit{clingo}'s incremental mode, future work could explore algorithms such as fixed-horizon queries, as discussed by \textcite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}, to potentially reduce the overhead of maintaining the solver state.

Additionally, the guess-and-check strategy could be further refined by implementing more adaptive fallbacks for instance.
Finally, the encodings could be generalized to better support conditional effects, derived predicates or axiom rules.

Ultimately, realizing efficient planning with ASP remains a challenging goal. By combining the structural optimizations identified in this thesis with more flexible solving strategies, ASP has the potential to become an even more competitive tool for general-purpose planning.
