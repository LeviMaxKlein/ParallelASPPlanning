\chapter{Introduction}\label{intro}
Automated planning is a fundamental area of Artificial Intelligence that deals with finding a sequence of actions to transition a system from an initial state to a desired goal state. 
Finding an efficient and effective plan is crucial in various applications and industries such as robotics and logistics. 
In general, planning problems are computationally challenging, often falling into NP-hard or even PSPACE-complete complexity classes.
Therefore, developing efficient algorithms and representations for planning tasks to reduce the search space and computation time is of great importance.

One of the strengths of Classical Planning is its domain independence. This means planning techniques can be used in many different fields without adjusting the core methods. This makes classical planning highly flexible and widely applicable.

\section{Introduction to Planning Task and Parallel Planning}

Before discussing the specific encodings and optimizations for parallel planning, we establish a precise mathematical model of the planning problem.

\begin{defi}[Multi-valued planning task]
    A STRIPS-like multivalued planning task according to \parencite{Helmert2006} is given by a 4 tuple $\langle \mathcal{F}, s_0, s_*, \mathcal{O} \rangle$, where:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\mathcal{F}$ is a finite set of state variables, also called \textbf{fluents}. Each $x \in \mathcal{F}$ is associated with a finite domain $\dom(x)$. In any given state, a fluent $x$ is assigned exactly one value $v \in \dom(x)$.
      \item $s_0$, the \textbf{initial state}, is a total function,, s.t. $s_0(x) \in \dom(x)$ for each $x \in \mathcal{F}$, i.e. a function that maps each fluent to a value from its domain.
      \item $s_*$, the \textbf{goal conditions}, is a partial function, s.t. $s_*(x) \in \dom(x)$, for each $x \in \dom(s_*)$ i.e. a function that specifies the desired values for a subset of fluents.
      \item $\mathcal{O}$ is a finite set of operators, also called \textbf{actions}. An action $a \in \mathcal O$ is a tuple $\langle a^c, a^e \rangle$, where $a^c$ is the \textit{precondition} and $a^e$ is the \textit{postcondition} of $a$. 
      Both $a^c$ and $a^e$ are partial functions, such that $a^c(x) \in \dom(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(a^c)$ and $a^e(x) \in \dom(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(a^e)$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{defi}

\begin{remark}
  In many implementations, actions also include \textbf{conditional effects} \textit{eff} $=\langle cond, x, v \rangle$, where the fluent $x \in \mathcal F$ is assigned value $v \in \dom(x)$ iff $cond$, a partial function called the \textit{condition} such that $cond(x) \in \dom(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(cond)$, holds in the current state.
  However, for the definitions and ASP encodings discussed in this thesis, we adopt a simplified view where effect conditions are implicitly assumed to be satisfied (i.e., mapping any effect on $x$ to $a^e(x)$ regardless of its condition). Thus, $a^e$ denotes the union of all variable assignments an action can potentially trigger.
\end{remark}

\begin{notation}
  Formally, the fact that a fluent $X$ is assigned the value $V$ in a state $s$ is denoted by $s(X) = V$.
  For readability, we use the compact notation $X=V$ to denote this assignment when the reference to the specific state $s$ is implicit from the context.
\end{notation}

\begin{example}
  Let us illustrate the definitions with a small example. The plan is to leave the house by opening the door, going outside, and finally closing the door.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{F} = \{door, location\}$ with $\dom(door) {=} \{open, closed\}$, $\dom(location) {=} \{inside, outside\}$
    \item Initial state: $s_0 = \{door{=}closed, location{=}inside\}$
    \item Goal: $s_* = \{door{=}closed, location{=}outside\}$
    \item Actions: $\mathcal{O} = \{openDoor, closeDoor, goOut\}$, where
    \vspace{-1em}
    \begin{align*}
      openDoor &= \langle\{door{=}closed\}, \{door{=}open\}\rangle\\
      closeDoor &= \langle\{door{=}open\}, \{door{=}closed\}\rangle\\
      goOut &= \langle\{location{=}inside, door{=}open\}, \{location{=}outside\}\rangle
    \end{align*}
  \end{itemize}
\end{example}

Intuitively, fluents can be understood as the dynamic properties that describe the configuration of the world. In our running example, this world is characterized by the \textit{location} of the actor and the status of the \textit{door}. The initial state captures the complete snapshot of the world at the beginning. This stands in contrast to the goal conditions, which represent the desired outcome of the planning task. Unlike the initial state, the goal is typically partial, meaning we only specify the values we explicitly care about, leaving other variables undefined. Finally, actions define the transition logic of the system: they describe how the world changes, where the precondition establishes the necessary requirements for the action to be applicable, and the postcondition specifies the resulting effects.

With these concepts in place, we now formally define the state transition function, which dictates how applying an action transforms one state into another.

\begin{defi}[Successor state]
  Let $s$ be state and $a \in \mathcal{O}$ an action.\\
  The \textit{successor state} $o(a,s)$, obtained by applying action $a$ in state $s$, is defined if the precondtion of $a$ holds, i.e., $a^c(x) = s(x)$ for all $x \in \dom(a^c)$. Then
  $$
  s'(x) = o(a,s) = \begin{cases} a^e(x) & \text{if } x \in \dom(a^e) \\ s(x) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
  $$ 
\end{defi}
\begin{remark}
  Let $A_n = \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$ be a sequence of actions with length n. We define the application of $A_n$ to a state $s$ recursevily by
  $$
    o(A_n,s) = o(a_n,o(A_{n-1},s))
  $$
  if $o(A_i, s)$ is defined for all $1 \le i \le n$.
\end{remark}

We can represent these transitions in a directed graph, where each node correspond to a distinct state, while the edges represent the possible actions from one state into another. Consequently, a solution equates to finding a path through this graph. 
Graphically, this transition mechanism induces a directed graph structure known as the \textit{state space}.
In this representation, the nodes correspond to the distinct states of the world, while the edges represent the actions transforming one state into another.
Consequently, finding a solution equates to identifying a path through this graph.
Since this graph grows exponentially with the number of actions, efficiently searching through this state space is a central challenge in automated planning.

\begin{defi}[Sequential plan]
  A \textit{sequential plan} is a sequence of actions $A_n = \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$, s.t. 
  $s' = o(A_n,s_0)$ is defined and $s'(x) = s_*(x)$ for all $x \in \dom(s_*)$.
\end{defi}

\begin{example}
  Consider the planning task from the previous example. One possible sequential plan is $A = \langle openDoor, goOut, closeDoor \rangle$. Since
  \begin{align*}
    o(A, s_0) &= o(\langle openDoor, goOut, closeDoor\rangle, \{door{=}closed, location{=}inside\})))\\
    &= o(\langle goOut, closeDoor \rangle, \{door{=}open, location{=}inside\}))\\
    &= o(closeDoor, \{door{=}open, location{=}outside\})\\
    &= \{door{=}closed, location{=}outside\} = s_*
  \end{align*}
\end{example}
\hspace{-1.5em}

While the sequential approach is intuitive, it is often inefficient searching through the whole state space.
A possible way to reduce the horizon required to find a solution is to allow multiple actions to be executed simultaneously, forming a parallel plan.
However, arbitrary sets of actions cannot simply be executed simultaneously, since conflicts may arise if actions interfere with each other.
The most fundamental requirement for a set of actions to be valid is that they must agree on their postcondition.
If two actions executing in parallel attempt to assign different values to the same variable, the resulting state would be undefined.
To prevent this ambiguity, we introduce the concept of \textit{confluence}.


\begin{defi}[confluent]
  Let $A = \{a_1, \dots, a_n\} \subseteq \mathcal{O}$ be a set of actions.\\
  $A$ is \textit{confluent} $\logeq a_i^e(x) = a_j^e(x)$ for all $1 \le i < j \le n$ and each $x \in \dom(a_i^e) \cap \dom(a_j^e)$
\end{defi}

\begin{remark}
  $A$ is confluent $\Leftrightarrow B$ is confluent for all $B \subseteq A$
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
  In our initial example, the set $A = \{openDoor, goOut\}$ is confluent because the domains of their effects are disjoint. Similarly, $\{closeDoor, goOut\}$ is confluent.
  
  To illustrate more complex scenarios, we extend our example with two fluents, $light$ and $key$, where $\dom(light) = \dom(key) = \{0, 1\}$, and two corresponding actions:
  \begin{align*}
    lightsOut &= \langle \{location{=}inside, light{=}1\}, \{light{=}0\} \rangle \\
    getKey &= \langle \{location{=}inside, key{=}0\}, \{key{=}1\} \rangle
  \end{align*}
  Additionally, we modify the precondition of $openDoor$ to require the key ($openDoor^c = \{door{=}closed, key{=}1\}$) and update the goal to $s_* = \{location{=}outside, door{=}closed, light{=}0\}$.
  
  In this extended setting, the set $A' = \{getKey, openDoor, goOut, lightsOut\}$ is also a confluent set of actions. Even though the actions are semantically related, their effects target different variables ($key, door, location, light$), so no conflicts in their effects occur.
\end{example}

The concept of confluence ensures that the simultaneous execution of actions within a set $A$ does not lead to contradictory state updates regarding the postcondition of the actions $a \in A$. 
However, ensuring consistent effects is only one part of the problem, since we also need to consider the preconditions of the actions. Unlike in sequential planning, where preconditions are simply checked against the current state, parallel planning allows for different interpretations of precondition dependencies.
These interpretations lead to the different parallel representations of sequential plans, which have been investigated in the literature \parencite{Dimopoulos1997, RINTANEN20061031, Wehrle2007} and are specifically implemented and tested using Answer Set Programming in \parencite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}.


\begin{defi}[Parallel representations]
  Let $s$ be a state and $A = \{a_1, \dots, a_n\}$ be a confluent set of actions. Then $A$ is called:
  \begin{align*}
    \text{(i)}\quad & \text{\textit{$\forall$-step serializable} in $s$} &&\logeq \quad 
      \parbox[t]{0.55\textwidth}{
        $o(\langle a_{\pi(1)}, \dots, a_{\pi(n)} \rangle, s)$ is defined \\ 
        for \textbf{all} permutations $\pi$
      } \\[1em]
    \text{(ii)}\quad & \text{\textit{$\exists$-step serializable} in $s$} &&\logeq \quad 
      \parbox[t]{0.55\textwidth}{
        $o(\langle a_{\pi(1)}, \dots, a_{\pi(n)} \rangle, s)$ is defined \\
        for \textbf{some} permutation $\pi$ and\\
        $a^c(x) = s(x)$ for each $a \in A$, $x \in \dom(a^c)$
      } \\[1em]
    \text{(iii)}\quad & \text{\textit{relaxed $\exists$-step serializable} in $s$} &&\logeq \quad 
      \parbox[t]{0.55\textwidth}{
        $o(\langle a_{\pi(1)}, \dots, a_{\pi(n)} \rangle, s)$ is defined \\
        for \textbf{some} permutation $\pi$
      }
  \end{align*}
\end{defi}

\begin{remark}
  $A$ is $\forall$-step serializable $\Rightarrow$ $A$ is $\exists$-step serializable $\Rightarrow$ $A$ is relaxed $\exists$-step serializable.
\end{remark}

Each parallel representation has different restrictions on how the preconditions of the actions in $A$ are satisfied with $\forall$-step being the most restrictive and relaxed $\exists$-step being the least restrictive. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$\forall$-step serialization} requires strong independence: Since the set must be executable in \textit{any} order, no action is allowed to invalidate the preconditions of another action in the same set and since any action $a \in A$ must be executable at the start, all preconditions have to be satisfied in the current state $s$.
    
    \item \textbf{$\exists$-step serialization} relaxes the independence. Preconditions must still be satisfied in $s$ (i.e., actions cannot enable each other). However, conflicts are allowed: One action $a \in A$ may destroy the precondition of another action $a' \in A$, provided that there exists \textit{some} valid execution order where $a$ comes after $a'$.
    
    \item \textbf{Relaxed $\exists$-step serialization} is the least restrictive. It drops the requirement that preconditions must hold in $s$. An action $a \in A$ can establish the preconditions required by another action $a' \in A$, as long as they can be ordered such that the $a$ comes before $a'$.
\end{itemize}

Based on these definitions of valid parallel steps, we can now define the corresponding parallel plans.

\begin{defi}[Parallel Plans]
  Let $A = \langle A_1, \dots, A_m \rangle$ be a sequence of confluent sets of actions. $A$ is a \textit{$\forall$-step, $\exists$-step or relaxed $\exists$-step plan} iff
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $s_m(x) = s_*(x)$ for each $x \in \dom(s_*)$
    \item $A_i$ is $\forall$-step, $\exists$-step or relaxed $\exists$-step serializable in $s_{i-1}$ for $1 \le i \le m$, where \\
    \[
      s_i(x) = \begin{cases} a^e(x) & \text{if } x \in \dom(a^e) \\ s_{i-1}(x) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
    \]
    for each $a \in A_i$.
    
  \end{itemize}
\end{defi}

\begin{example}
  In our modified example we have:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\forall$-step plan: $\langle\{getKey, lightsOut\}, \{openDoor\}, \{goOut\}, \{closeDoor\}\rangle$
    \item $\exists$-step plan: $\langle\{getKey, lightsOut\}, \{openDoor\}, \{goOut, closeDoor\}\rangle$
    \item relaxed $\exists$-step plan: $\langle\{getKey, lightsOut, openDoor, goOut\}, \{closeDoor\}\rangle$
  \end{itemize}
\end{example}

While the concept of a sequential plan is straightforward, we are effectively dealing with transitions of the form $s \xrightarrow{a} s'$. 
In parallel planning, however, we generalize this transition to allow sets of actions, i.e., $s \xrightarrow{A} s'$, effectively merging multiple actions together into a single one.

\section{Introduction to ASP}
Answer Set Programming (ASP) is a declarative problem-solving approach oriented towards difficult (primarily NP-hard) search problems. Unlike imperative programming, where the programmer specifies the control flow to compute a solution, ASP follows a declarative paradigm: the problem is described as a logic program such that its valid models—called \textit{answer sets}—correspond to the solutions of the original problem. This methodology allows for a clean separation between the problem description (the logic program) and the solving algorithms (implemented by ASP systems like \textit{clingo}).

The theoretical foundation of ASP lies in the stable model semantics of logic programming \parencite{gel88}. To formally define these programs, we first introduce their basic building blocks. The most fundamental units are \textit{atoms} of the form $p(t_1, \dots, t_n)$ or $p$, which can either be true or false. 
These atoms are combined to form literals. A literal is defined as either an atom $A$ (a positive literal) or its default negation $not$ $A$ (a negative literal). Here, $not$ denotes negation as failure, meaning that $not$ $A$ is considered true if no proof for $A$ can be derived from the program.


\subsection*{Logic Programs}
The head of a rule or fact is an atom, which has the same syntatic form as a constant or a function, i.e. $prec(X,V,t)$.
The body consists of a (possible empty) list of literals $L_1, \dots L_n$. Each $L_i$ is of the form $A$ or $not~A$, where $A$ is an atom and $not$ the default negation.
The ''$:$-'' operator can be read as ''$\Leftarrow$'', therefore a rule can be interpreted as "if all literals in the body are true, then the head is true".
A fact is a rule without a body, meaning that the head atom is unconditionally true. An integrity constraint is a rule without a head, that filters solution candidates, meaning that all the literal in its body must not be satisfied, i.e. at least one literal in the body must be false.
A set of atoms is called a $model$ of a logic program, if all facts, rules and integrity constraints are satisfied. 
Atoms are considered true if they are in the model, otherwise they are considered false.
In ASP, a model is called an $answer set$ or $stable model$, if every atom in the model has an acyclic derivation of the program.


Based on these definitions, we can specify the structure of the programs used to model planning tasks. A \textit{normal logic program} consists of a finite set of rules, facts, and integrity constraints. These constructs generally follow the form:
\begin{align*}
   \textbf{Fact:} & \quad A_0. \\
   \textbf{Rule:} & \quad A_0 :\text{- } L_1, \dots, L_n. \\
   \textbf{Constraint:} & \quad \phantom{A_0} :\text{- } L_1, \dots, L_n.
\end{align*}
The \textit{head} of a rule is an atom $A_0$. The \textit{body} consists of a (possibly empty) conjunction of literals $L_1, \dots, L_n$. 
The symbol ``\texttt{:-}'' stands for logical implication ($\leftarrow$) and allows the rule to be interpreted as: "if all literals in the body are true, then the head must be true".

We distinguish three types of rules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{fact} is a rule with an empty body, implying that the head atom $A_0$ is unconditionally true.
    \item A \textbf{rule} derives the head $A_0$ if the body is satisfied.
    \item An \textbf{integrity constraint} is a rule with an empty head. It serves as a filter for solution candidates: At least one literal in the body must be false,otherwise the candidate model is rejected.
\end{itemize}

A set of atoms is called a model of a logic program if it satisfies all rules, facts, and integrity constraints. Atoms are considered true iff
they are contained in the model. In ASP, a model is called an answer set if every atom in the
model has an acyclic derivation from the program.
Based on these semantics, we can now translate the formal definition of a planning task into an ASP logic program. 
The problem instance is represented purely by facts:
the initial state $s_0$ is defined via \texttt{init/2}, the goal $s_*$ via \texttt{goal/2}, and the pre- and postconditions of actions via \texttt{prec/3} and \texttt{post/3} respectively.

The \textbf{\textit{base}} subprogram (Line 2) defines the initial state $s_0$, grounding the fluent values at time step $0$. The predicate $holds(X,V,t)$ is used to represent fluents $X \in \mathcal{F}$ that have value $V\in \dom(X)$ at timestep $t$.

The \textbf{\textit{check(t)}} subprogram (Line 4) is a parametrized subprogram starting at $t=0$. The integrity constraint checks if the goald condition is met, i.e. $s_*(X)=s_t(X)$ for each $X\in \dom(s_*)$. The external atom $query(t)$ ensures this check is only active for the current search horizon $t$.

The \textbf{\textit{step(t)}} subprogram (Line 6-9) is grounded for each timestep $t$ starting at $t=1$. 
Note that postconditions of applied actions in timestep $t$ become effective in $t$, while their preconditions have to hold in $t-1$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Line 6 generates the state candidates for timestep $t$, assigning every fluent $X$ exactly one value $V$ from its domain.
    \item Line 7 generates arbitrary sets of actions $occurs(A,t)$ for the current timestep. These actions and the generated state must obey the constraints in Lines 8 and 9.
    \item Line 8 ensures that the postcondition of an occurring action is actually applied in the state. This also asserts the confluence of the set of actions: if the postconditions of two actions have different values for the same fluent ($V = a^e(X) \ne a'^e(X) = V'$), the uniqueness of the value in the choice rule (Line 6) would be violated.
    \item Line 9 guarantees that fluents not affected by any action keep their value from the previous timestep. It rejects any change in a fluent's value that is not explained by an action.
\end{itemize}
In summary, Listing \ref{core} establishes the state transitions consistent with the postcondition of the chosen actions. 
However, it does not yet verify the \textit{applicability} of these actions regarding their preconditions, nor does it enforce the specific serializability requirements (e.g., acyclicity) of the parallel semantics.

The extension for $\forall$-step plans is shown in Listing \ref{forall}.
Line 1 is identical to the sequential encoding, ensuring that the preconditions of all chosen actions are satisfied in the previous state $s_{t-1}$.
To create a valid $\forall$-step plan, we must ensure that each set of actions is executable in \textit{any} order. 
Since Line 8 of the common core (Listing \ref{core}) already enforces confluence, the remaining requirement is that no action is allowed to invalidate the preconditions of another action in the same set.
We distinguish two types of interactions between an action $a$ and a fluent $X$ in its precondition ($\dom(a^c)$):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The action has $X$ in its preconditions fluent $X$ in its precondition but does not change it, (i.e. $X \in \dom(a^c) \cap \dom(a^e)$ and $a^c(X) = a^e(X)$ or $X \in \dom(a^c)$ and $X \notin \dom(a^e)$). 
  \item The action has fluent $X$ in its preconditions and changes its value (i.e., $X \in \dom(a^e)$ and $a^c(X) \ne a^e(X)$). 
\end{enumerate}
Line 2 handles case 1 by ensuring that $X$ has to remained unchanged. 
However, case 2 requires strict isolation of these kinds of actions. 
If an action $a$ invalidates its own precondition, no other action $a'$ in the same timestep is allowed to modify $X$. 
If such an action $a'$ existed, the execution order $\langle a', a \rangle$ would fail because $a'$ would change $X$, invalidating the precondition of $a$.
To enforce this, Line 3 marks such actions using the predicate \texttt{single}$(X,t)$. 
The integrity constraint in Line 4 then ensures that for any fluent $X$, at most one such action occurs per time step, preventing any interference.

\section{Proof of Correctness}
In order to prove the correctness of the presented encondings, \cite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} defines the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $B$ is the rule in Line 1 of Listing \ref{core}.
  \item $Q(t)$ is the integrity constraint in the check program directive (Line 5) of Listing \ref{core}.
  \item $S(t)$ are the rules and integrity constraint in the step program directive (Lines 6-9) of Listing \ref{core}.
  \item $S^p(t)$ is the extension for the specific plans,
        where $p = s$ for Listing \ref{seq}, $p=\forall$ for Listing \ref{forall}, $p=\exists$ for Listing \ref{exists},
        $p=E$ for Listing \ref{exists_alt} and $p=R$ for Listing \ref{relaxed_exists}.
  \item $I$ is the set of facts representing a planning task $\langle \mathcal{F}, s_0, s_*, \mathcal{O} \rangle$.
  \item $\langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$ is a sequence of actions.
  \item $\langle A_1, \dots, A_m \rangle$ is a a sequence of sets of actions.
  \item $P^p(n) := I \cup B \cup Q(0) \cup \bigcup_{t=1}^{n} \left( Q(t) \cup S(t) \cup S^p(t) \right) \cup \{\texttt{query}(n). \}$, where $p \in \{s, \forall, \exists, E, R\}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{theo}[Correctness of sequential plans \parencite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}]
  \label{theo:1}
  \phantom{}\\
  $\langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$ is a sequential plan $\iff$ $P^s(n)$ has a stable model $M$, s.t. \\
  $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs}(a,t) \in M \} = \{\langle a_t, t \rangle \mid 1 \le t \le n \}$.
\end{theo}

\begin{proof}
  We prove both directions separately.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item[($\Rightarrow$):] 
    Assume $A_n := \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$ is a sequential plan. \\
    We construct a candidate set $M$ based on the plan execution and show it is indeed a stable model of $P^s(n)$.
    The rule in B ensures that \texttt{holds}(X,V,0) $= s_0(X)$ for each $X \in \mathcal F$. The choice rule (Line 5 of Listing \ref{seq}) enforces that for any $0 \le t \le n$ and $X \in \mathcal F$, exactly one value $V$ is assigned, i.e., $|\{V \mid \texttt{holds}(X,V,t) \in M\}| = 1$. 
    Moreover, the integrity constraints (Lines 8 and 9) in $S(t)$ dictate the transition logic between states. 
    For any $1 \le t \le n$, the value $V$ of a fluent $X$ is determined by:
    \[
      \texttt{holds}(X,V,t) \leftrightarrow \begin{cases} V = a_t^e(X) & \text{if } X \in \text{dom}(a_t^e) \\ V = s_{t-1}(X) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
    \]
    Therefore, the set of \texttt{holds}-atoms in any stable model must satisfy:
    \begin{equation}
      \{ \langle X, V, t \rangle \mid \texttt{holds}(X, V, t) \in M \}  = \{ \langle X, V, t \rangle \mid X \in \mathcal{F}, V {=} o(A_n, s_0)(X), 0 \le t \le n \}
      \label{eq:holds-equivalence}
    \end{equation}
    Since the action sequence is fixed by the plan assumption ($\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs}(a,t) \in M \} = \{\langle a_t, t \rangle \mid 1 \le t \le n \}$), the only candidate for a stable model $P^s(n)$ is:
    \begin{align*}
      M = I &\cup \{ \texttt{occurs}(a_t, t) \mid 1 \le t \le n \} \\
            &\cup \{\texttt{holds}(X, V, t) \mid (X, V, t) \text{ satisfies } \eqref{eq:holds-equivalence}\}    \cup \{\texttt{query}(n)\}.
    \end{align*}
     By the definition of a sequential plan, $o(a_t, s_{t-1})$ is defined for all $1 \le t \le n$, and the goal condition $s_n(X) = s_*$ for each $X \in \dom(s_*)$ is met. Thus, the integrity constraint regarding preconditions (Line 1, Listing \ref{seq}) and the goal check (Line 4, Listing \ref{core}) are satisfied. Since $M$ is a set satisfying all rules and constraints, it is indeed a stable model of $P^s(n)$.

  \item[($\Leftarrow$):]
    Assume that $M$ is a stable model of $P^s(n)$ s.t. $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs(a,t)} \in M \} = \{\langle a_t, t \rangle \mid 1 \le t \le n \}$. \\
    We prove by induction over $t$, that for each $1 \le t \le n$, the following holds:
    \begin{equation}
      \{\langle X, V \rangle \mid \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \in M \} = \{\langle X, V \rangle \mid X \in \mathcal F, V=o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_{t}\rangle, s_0)(X)\}
      \label{eq:holds-equivalence2}
    \end{equation}
    \textit{Base case }$t=0$: \\
    The rule in B directly implies that $\{\langle X, V \rangle \mid \texttt{holds(X, V, 0)} \in M \} = \{\langle X, V \rangle \mid X \in \mathcal F, s_0(X) = V\}$,
    satisfying the equation for $t=0$. \\
    \textit{Induction step }$t-1 \mapsto t$: \\
    Assume the hypothesis holds for $t-1$, i.e., the state at $t-1$ corresponds to $s_{t-1} = o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_{t-1} \rangle, s_0)$.. The integrity constraint in Line 1 of Listing \ref{seq} in $S^s(t)$ ensures that the successor state $s_t = o(a_{t}, o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_{t-1}\rangle, s_0))$ is defined and the choice rule in Line 6 of Listing \ref{core}, together with the integrity constraints in $S(t)$ guarantee that 
    \[
    \{\langle X, V \rangle \mid \texttt{holds(X,V,t)} \in M\} = \{\langle X, V \rangle \mid X \in \mathcal F, V = o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_{t} \rangle, s_0)(X)\}.
    \]
    Thus \eqref{eq:holds-equivalence2} holds for each $1 \le t \le n$.
    Given \texttt{query(n)}, the integrity constraint in $Q(n)$ yields that $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle, s_0) = s_*(X)$ for each $X \in \dom(s_*)$ and therefore $\langle a_1, \dots a_n\rangle$ is a sequential plan. 
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{theo}[Correctness of parallel plans \parencite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}]
  \phantom{} \\
  $\langle A_1, \dots, A_m \rangle$ is a $\forall$-/$exists$-/relaxed $exists$-step plan $\iff P^p(m)$ has a stable model M s.t.,
  $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs}(a,t) \in M \} = \{\langle a, t \rangle \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \}$ with $p \in \{\forall, \exists, R\}$.
\end{theo}

\begin{proof}
  We prove both directions separately.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item[($\Rightarrow$):]
      Assume $\mathcal A = \langle A_1, \dots, A_m \rangle$ is a $\forall$-/$\exists$-/\textit{relaxed} $\exists$-step plan.
      For each $1 \le t \le m$, let $s_t$ be the state defined by applying the set $A_t$ to $s_{t-1}$. Due to the confluence of $A_t$, $s_t$ is well-defined as:
      \[
        s_t(X) = \begin{cases} 
          a^e(X) & \text{for each } a \in A_t \text{ and } X \in \dom(a^e) \\ 
          s_{t-1}(X) & \text{otherwise (inertia)}
        \end{cases}
      \]
      Analogous to the proof of Theorem \ref{theo:1}, consider any stable model $M$ of $P^p(m)$ such that the action occurrences match the plan, i.e. $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs}(a,t) \in M\} = \{\langle a,t \rangle \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \}$.
      The fact rule in $B$, together with the choice rule and integrity constraints in $S(t)$, dictates the values of all fluents based on these actions. Thus, the set of \texttt{holds}-atoms in $M$ is determined as:
      \[
        \{\langle X,V,t\rangle \mid \texttt{holds}(X,V,t) \in M\} = \{\langle X,V,t\rangle \mid X \in \mathcal F, V = s_t(X), 0 \le t \le m\}.
      \]
      We now distinguish the different cases of parallel plans: \\


      \textbf{Case 1:} $\mathcal A$ is a $\forall$-step plan. \\
      For $1 \le t \le m$, the atom \texttt{single}$(X,t)$ defined in $S^\forall(t)$ (Line 2 of Listing \ref{forall}) is derived iff there exists some action $a \in A_t$ that invalidates its own precondition, i.e., $X \in \dom(a^c) \cap \dom(a^e)$ and $a^c(X) \ne a^e(X)$. 
      Hence, $P^\forall(m)$ cannot have any stable model $M$ apart from
      \begin{align*}
        M = I &\cup \{\texttt{occurs}(a,t) \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m\} \\
              &\cup \{\texttt{holds}(X,V,t) \mid X \in \mathcal F, V = s_t(X), 0 \le t \le m\} \\
              &\cup \{\texttt{single}(X,t) \mid a \in A_t, X \in \dom(a^c) \cap \dom(a^e), a^c(X) \ne a^e(X), 1 \le t \le m \} \\
              &\cup \{\texttt{query}(m).\}
      \end{align*}
      such that $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs}(a,t) \in M\} = \{\langle a, t \rangle \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m\}$.
      
      It remains to show that $M$ satisfies the integrity constraints in $S^\forall(t)$.
      Since for any $1 \le t \le m$, the successor state $s_t= o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined for \textit{any} sequence $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ with $\{a_1, \dots, a_k\} = A_t$, no action can interfere with the precondition of another action.
      Consequently, if \texttt{single}$(X,t)$ is derived (meaning an action invalidates its own precondition on $X$), no other action can modify $X$, as this would create a conflict in at least one permutation. Therefore, the integrity constraint restricting the count of actions affecting $X$ alongside \texttt{single}$(X,t)$ is satisfied.
      Analogously, the constraint requiring preconditions to remain stable (Line 1 of Listing \ref{forall}) is satisfied since $s_{t-1}(X)=a^c(X)$ for each $a \in A_t$, $X \in dom(a^c)$ and $1\le t \le m$.
      One can check that the remaining rules and integrity constraints in $S(t)$ are satisfied as well, so $M$ is a stable model of $P^\forall(m)$. \\


      \textbf{Case 2:} $\mathcal A$ is a $\exists$-step plan. \\
      We analyze the encodings $P^\exists$ and $P^E$ separately, starting with the acyclicity-based encoding $P^E$.

      \textit{Analysis of }$P^E$: \\
      By definition of an $\exists$-step plan, for every timestep $1 \le t \le m$, there is a sequence of actions $\langle a_1, \dots a_k\rangle$ with $\{a_1, \dots, a_k\} = A_t$. A valid serialization requires that no action invalidates the precondition of a subsequent action.
      Formally, let $G_t = (A_t, E_t)$ be the conflict graph for timestep $t$, where an edge represents a destroyed precondition:
      \[
        E_t = \{ (a, a') \in A_t \times A_t \mid a \ne a', \exists X \in \dom(a^e) \cap \dom(a'^c) : a^e(X) \ne a'^c(X) \}.
      \]
      Since a valid serialization exists, $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ constitutes a topological ordering of $G_t$, i.e. there exists a sequence of applied actions. A directed graph possesses a topological ordering iff it is a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG). Thus, $G_t$ must be acyclic.

      Now consider the candidate model $M$ constructed as in Case 1:
      \begin{align*}
        M = I &\cup \{\texttt{occurs}(a,t) \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \} \\
              &\cup \{\texttt{holds}(X,V,t) \mid X \in \mathcal F, V=s_t(X), 0 \le t \le m\} \cup \{\texttt{query}(m)\}.
      \end{align*}
      The \texttt{\#edge} directive in Listing \ref{exists_alt} (Lines 2-3) generates an edge between two occurring actions $(a,t)$ and $(a',t)$ exactly if the condition for $E_t$ is met. Consequently, the set of edges declared in the ASP model corresponds exactly to the union of all conflict graphs $\bigcup_{t=1}^m E_t$.
      Since each $G_t$ is acyclic and the graphs for different timesteps $1 \le t < t' \le m$ are vertex-disjoint ($(a,t), \ne (a, t')$), the overall graph induced by $M$ is acyclic.
      Therefore, the acyclicity constraint is satisfied, and $M$ is a stable model of $P^E(m)$. \\
      \textit{Analysis of }$P^\exists$: \\
      The rules defining \texttt{apply} and \texttt{ready} in $S^\exists(t)$ (Lines 2-5 in Listing \ref{exists}) effectively traverse the dependency graph $G_t$.
      Since $G_t$ is acyclic, there exists a topological ordering of the actions. The derivation of atoms proceeds along this ordering: Actions with no dependencies (sinks in $G_t$) are derived as \texttt{apply} first, making them \texttt{ready}. This, in turn, satisfies the conditions for their predecessors. 
      Because $G_t$ is finite and acyclic, this inductive process reaches a fixpoint where \texttt{apply}$(a,t)$ and \texttt{ready}$(a,t)$ hold for all $a \in \mathcal O$ and $1 \le t \le m$.
      Consequently, the integrity constraint requiring all actions to be ready (Line 6 in Listing \ref{exists}) is satisfied.
      Thus, $P^\exists(m)$ extends the base model $M$ to:
      \[
        M' = M \cup \{\texttt{apply}(a,t) \mid a \in \mathcal O, 1 \le t \le m\} \cup \{\texttt{ready}(a,t) \mid a \in \mathcal O, 1 \le t \le m \}
      \]
      such that $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs}(a,t) \in M'\} = \{\langle a, t \rangle \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \}$.
      As established previously, the state transition rules are satisfied by $M$ (and thus by $M'$). Since the additional constraints in $P^\exists$ regarding circularity are also satisfied by the completeness of the \texttt{ready} derivation, $M'$ is indeed a stable model of $P^\exists(m)$. \medskip

      \textbf{Case 3:} $\mathcal A$ is a \textit{relaxed} $\exists$-step plan. \\
      For each $1 \le t \le m$, by definition of a relaxed $\exists$-step plan, there exists a serialization $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ of $A_t$ such that the sequential execution is valid. This means $o(a_i, o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_{i-1} \rangle, s_{t-1}))$ is defined for all $1 \le i \le k$.
      
      Consequently, the preconditions of any action $a_i$ are satisfied by the state resulting from the application of its predecessors in $\langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$.
      The rules in $S^R(t)$ (Listing \ref{relaxed_exists}) simulate this reachability:
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item The atom \texttt{reach}>$(X, V, t)$ is derived for all values $V$ that are either present in $s_{t-1}$ or produced by an applied action.
        \item The atom \texttt{apply}$(a, t)$ is derived for any action $a \in \mathcal O$ whose preconditions are satisfied by the reachable values.
        \item The atom \texttt{ready}$(a, t)$ is derived for all $a \in \mathcal O$ (either because they are applied, or because they do not occur).
      \end{enumerate} 
      Since the serialization $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ is valid, the least fixpoint of these rules guarantees that \texttt{apply}$(a,t)$ is derived for all $a \in A_t$.
      
      Thus, the stable model $M$ of $P^R(m)$ is:
      \begin{align*}
        M = I &\cup \{\texttt{occurs}(a,t) \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{holds}(X,V,t) \mid X \in \mathcal F, V=s_t(X), 0 \le t \le m \} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{reach}(X,V,t) \mid X \in \mathcal F, V \in \{s_{t-1}, s_t\}, 1 \le t \le m\} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{apply}(a,t) \mid a \in \mathcal O: a^c(X) \in \{s_{t-1}(X), s_t(X)\} \text{ for each } X \in \dom(a^c)\} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{ready}(a,t) \mid a \in \mathcal O, 1 \le t \le m\} \\
          &\cup \{\texttt{query}(m)\}
      \end{align*}
      Since $M$ contains $ready(a,t)$ for all actions, the integrity constraint in Line 7 of Listing \ref{relaxed_exists} is satisfied. Furthermore, all state transition constraints are met by the definition of $s_t$.
      Therefore, $M$ is a stable model of $P^R(m)$.
    \item[($\Leftarrow$):]
      Assume that $M$ is a stable model of $P^p(m)$ such that $\{\langle a, t \rangle \mid \texttt{occurs}(a,t) \in M \} = \{\langle a, t \rangle \mid a \in A_t, 1 \le t \le m \}$. \\
      For any $1 \le t \le m$, the choice rule in Line 6 of Listing \ref{core} ensures that the set of \texttt{holds}-atoms induces a state function $s_t$ defined by $s_t(X) = V \Leftrightarrow \texttt{holds}(X,V,t) \in M$.
      
      Moreover, the rules and integrity constraints in $S(t)$ dictate the state transition. Specifically, the integrity constraint in Line 8 of Listing \ref{core} ensure that for each $a \in A_t$ and $X \in \dom(a^e)$, $s_t(X) = a^e(X)$ holds. Since $M$ is a stable model, contradicting effects on the same variable are impossible (as they would violate the constraints), which implies that $A_t$ is confluent.
      For unaffected fluents, the integrity constraint in Line 9 of Listing \ref{core} yield $s_t(X) = s_{t-1}(X)$ for each $X \in \mathcal F \setminus \bigcup_{a \in A_t} \dom(a^e)$. 
      
      Finally, given the fact \texttt{query}(m), the integrity constraint in $Q(m)$ ensures that $s_m(X) = s_*(X)$ for all $X \in \dom(s_*)$, satisfying the goal condition.
      
      We now distinguish the different cases to show that $A_t$ is $\forall$-/$\exists$-/\textit{relaxed} $\exists$-step serializable in $s_{t-1}$ based on the specific constraints of each encoding.

      \textbf{Case 1: } To show: $A_t$ is $\forall$-step serializable. \\
      Let $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ be an arbitrary sequence of actions such that $\{a_1, \dots, a_k\}=A_t$.
      We prove by induction that for all $1 \le i \le k$, the transition $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_i \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined.
      \textit{Base case:} $i=1$: \\ 
      The integrity constraint checking preconditions (Line 1 of Listing \ref{forall}) guarantees that $a_1$ is applicable in $s_{t-1}$, i.e., $o(a_1, s_{t-1})$ is defined. \\

      \textit{Inductive step} $i \mapsto i+1$: \\ 
      Assume $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_i \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined. We must show that $a_{i+1}$ is applicable in the resulting intermediate state $s_{temp} = o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_i \rangle, s_{t-1})$.
      Consider any fluent $X$ in the precondition of $a_{i+1}$. 
      If $X \notin \bigcup_{1 \le j \le i} \dom(a_j^e)$, then no preceding action has modified $X$. Thus $s_{temp}(X) = s_{t-1}(X)$, and the precondition holds trivially because $a_{i+1}$ is applicable in $s_{t-1}$.
      Therefore, we focus on the critical case where $X \in \dom(a_{i+1}^c) \cap \bigcup_{1 \le j \le i} \dom(a_j^e)$.
      This implies there exists a preceding action $a_j$ that has a postcondition on $X$. We distinguish two cases based on the effect of $a_{i+1}$ itself:
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item $X \notin \dom(a_{i+1}^e)$: \\
        The integrity constraint in Line 2 of Listing \ref{forall} requires that if $a_{i+1}$ has a precondition on $X$ but no postcondition, the value of $X$ cannot change in the final state $s_t$. Since $a_j$ writes $X$, we have $s_t(X) = a_j^e(X)$. The constraint implies $s_{t-1}(X) = s_t(X)$, meaning $a_j$ effectively did not change the value relative to $s_{t-1}$. Thus $s_{temp}(X) = a_{i+1}^c(X)$.
        \item $X \in \dom(a_{i+1}^e)$: \\
        The integrity constraint in Line 4 of Listing \ref{forall} ensures that if an action ($a_{i+1}$) has a precondition on $X$ and sets a conflicting postcondition ($a^c(X) \neq a^e(X)$), it is marked as \texttt{single}. However, since another action $a_j$ also acts on $X$ (as established by the intersection), the integrity constraint in Line 5 would be violated.
        Since $M$ is a stable model, this implies that $\texttt{single}(X, t) \notin M$ and $s_t(X) = a_{i+1}^e(X) = a_{i+1}^c(X)=s_{t-1}(X)$ must be the case.
      \end{enumerate} 
      In both cases, $s_{temp}(X) = a_{i+1}^c(X)$, so $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_{i+1} \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined.
      Therefore, by induction, the entire sequence is valid, and $A_t$ is $\forall$-step serializable. \\

      \textbf{Case 2: } To show: $A_t$ is $\exists$-step serializable. \\
      The integrity constraint in Line 1 of Listing \ref{exists} guarantees that every action $a \in A_t$ is applicable in $s_{t-1}$, i.e., $o(a, s_{t-1})$ is defined.
      Furthermore, the integrity constraint in Line 6 ensures that $\texttt{ready}(a, t) \in M$ for each $a \in \mathcal O$ and the rules in Lines 4-5 that $\texttt{apply}(a,t) \in M$ for all $a \in A_t$.
      The rule defining \texttt{apply} (Lines 2-3) imposes a dependency: an action $a$ can only be applied if all actions $a'$ whose preconditions it invalidates are already \texttt{ready}.
      Since $M$ is a stable model, there exists a derivation sequence of the \texttt{apply} atoms that respects these dependencies. Let $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ be the sequence of actions in $A_t$ ordered by their derivation step.
      Consider any action $a_i$ in the sequence and a fluent $X$ in the precondition.
      Suppose that a preceding action $a_j$ ($j < i$) invalidates this precondition, i.e., $a_j^e(X) \neq a_i^c(X)$.
      By the definition of \texttt{apply} in the Lines 2-3, $a_j$ would have to wait for $a_i$ to become \texttt{ready} before it could be applied. This would imply that $a_i$ is derived before $a_j$, contradicting $j < i$.
      Therefore, for all $j < i$, $a_j$ does not invalidate $a_i$. Thus, $s_{t-1}(X)$ is either preserved ($s_{t-1}(X)=s_t(X)$) or modified confluently ($a_j^e(X) = a_i^c(X)$), ensuring $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined. \\

      Regarding the alternative encoding using the \texttt{\#edge} directive (Listing \ref{exists_alt}), the graph logic ensures serializability explicitly.
      For any $1 \le t \le m$, the directive defines the conflict graph $G_t = (A_t, E_t)$ where a directed edge $(a, a') \in E_t$ exists iff action $a$ invalidates a precondition of action $a'$. 
      Since $P^E(m)$ enforces that $G_t$ is acyclic, there exists a sequence $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k\rangle$ comprising the actions in $A_t$ such that no edge connects any predecessor to a successor. Formally, for any $a_i$ in the sequence, there is no $a_j$ with $j < i$ such that $(a_j, a_i) \in E_t$.
      This absence of destructive edges from the past implies that for any $X$ in the precondition, either no predecessor changes $X$ or it is modified consistently, i.e.
      $s_{t-1}(X) = a_i^c(X)$ or $a_j^e(X) = a_i^c(X)$.
      Thus, $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_i \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined for all $i$, and in particular $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined, verifying that $A_t$ is $\exists$-step serializable. \\

      \textbf{Case 3: } To show: $A_t$ is \textit{relaxed} $\exists$-step serializable. \\
      In view of the rule in Line 1 of Listing \ref{relaxed_exists}, a fluent value is reachable if $s_{t-1}(X)=V$, i.e., $reach(X, V, t) \in M$.
      The recursive structure of the rules for \texttt{apply} (Lines 3-4) and \texttt{reach} (Line 2) imposes a dependency ordering: An action $a$ is only derived as \texttt{apply}(a,t) if all its preconditions are already in the extension of the \texttt{reach} predicate.

      Since $M$ is a stable model, there exists a finite derivation sequence for all atoms in $M$. Let $\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle$ be the sequence of actions in $A_t$ ordered by the step in which their corresponding \texttt{apply} atom was derived (or satisfied).
      We prove that this sequence is a valid serialization.

      Consider any action $a_i$ in this sequence and any fluent $X$ in the precondition.
      The derivation of \texttt{apply}($a_i$,t) requires that \texttt{reach}($X, a_i^c(X), t$) was previously derived. By the definition of the \texttt{reach} rules, this value must originate either from:
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item The state $s_{t-1}$ (Line 1), which implies $s_{t-1}(X) = a_i^c(X)$, or
        \item The effect of a previously applied action $a_j$ (Line 2), which implies $a_j \in \{a_1, \dots, a_{i-1}\}$ and $a_j^e(X) = a_i^c(X)$.
      \end{enumerate}
      In both cases, the precondition of $a_i$ is satisfied by the state constructed from the initial state $s_{t-1}$ and the effects of the predecessors $\langle a_1, \dots, a_{i-1} \rangle$.
      Thus, $o(\langle a_1, \dots, a_k \rangle, s_{t-1})$ is defined.
  \end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\section{Pipeline Overview}
\begin{figure}[H]

  \caption{Pipeline for testing the different planning encodings}

To test and compare the different parallel planning encodings against each other and against the sequential planning encoding, we set up a python pipeline using the lab framework \parencite{seipp-et-al-zenodo2017}
and the unofficial collection of (sequential) IPC benchmark instances from the downward project \footnote{\url{https://github.com/aibasel/downward-benchmarks}}. \\
Starting at a planning task in the Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL), we first preprocess it using cpddl \parencite{fiser_2025_16423302} that converts it into a finite domain representation (FDR).
Plasp then takes the FDR as input and translates it into ASP facts representing the planning task. Further information of this step can be found in \parencite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}.
Using clingo with a modified version of the incremental encodings presented above together with the facts from the output of plasp, we can then compute sequential, $\forall$-step, $\exists$-step or relaxed $\exists$-step plans as stable models.
Finally, to validate the given plan, we postprocess the parallel representation back into a sequential plan, using the postprocess encoding provided by plasp and validate the plan with VAL \footnote{\url{https://github.com/KCL-Planning/VAL}}.
Each step has some optimizations and configurations that will be explained in the following sections.

To use the presented encodings in our pipeline, we had to make some small changes to the original encodings from \parencite{DIMOPOULOS_2019}.

\section{Optimizations}
\subsection{Mutexes}
\begin{defi}[mutex and mutex groups]
  A set of facts $M \subseteq \mathcal F$ is called 
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item a \textit{mutex} $~~~~~~~\logeq$ for all reachable states $s: M \not\subseteq s$.
    \item a \textit{mutex group} $\logeq$ for all reachable states $s: | M \cap s | \le 1$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{defi}
\noindent In other words, a mutex is a set of fluents that can never be true at the same time in any reachable state,
while a mutex group is a set of fluents where at most one fluent can be true at the same time.
Mutexes and mutex groups can be used to optimize the planning encodings by reducing the search space.
For this, we extend our endcoding in Listing \ref{core} with the following rules:
\begin{listing}[H]
  \caption{Expansion for mutexes}
  \begin{minted}[frame=single,framesep=2mm,baselinestretch=1.2,linenos]{prolog}
    #program base.
    ...
    mutex(G,X) :- mutexGroup(G), contains(G,X,V), fluent(X,V).
    mutex(G)   :- mutexGroup(G), #count{X : mutex(G,X)} > 1.
    :- mutex(G), #count{X,V : holds(X,V,0), contains(G,X,V)} > 1.
    ...
    #program step(t).
    ...
    :- mutex(G), #count{X,V : holds(X,V,t), contains(G,X,V)} > 1.
    ...
  \end{minted}
  \label{mutex}
\end{listing}
\noindent Here, the mutex group G is given by plasp as facts of the form $mutexGroup/1$ and $contains/3$.
In the base program, we first define which fluents belong to a mutex group (Line 3), where a group must contain at least two fluents (Line 4).
The integrity constraint in Line 5 ensures that no more than one fluent of each mutex group holds in the initial state and the integrity constraint in Line 9 ensures the same for each state in timestep t.

\subsection{Guess and Check}
An optimization specific to the (relaxed) $\exists$-step encodings is the guess and check approach. 
The general idea is to first guess a solution without looking for actions that circularly invalidate each other and afterwards check if the guessed solution has a cycle.
Formally, we encode the problem by a pair of programs $\langle G, C \rangle$ where $G$ is the guessing program and $C$ is the checking program. 
Stable models $M$ of $G$ yields a solution if $C \cap M$ is $unsatisfiable$. 
For making "systematic" guesses, \parencite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} proposes to use the core program ($S(t)$) in Listing \ref{core} 
together with the integrity constraint in Line 1 of Listing \ref{seq}, \ref{forall} or \ref{exists} ($B$) and the facts representing a planning task.
Thus, a stable model $M$ of $G$ is s.t. the pre- and postconditons hold for all actions applied in parallel (Line 8 of Listing \ref{core} and Line 1 of Listing \ref{seq}, \ref{forall} or \ref{exists}).
and the integrity constraint in Line 8 of Listing \ref{core} also ensures confluence of applied actions. 
The only thing remained to be checked is if actions circularly invalidate each other, i.e. $a^e(X) \ne \tilde a^c(X)$ and $\tilde a^e(\tilde X) \ne \tilde a^c(\tilde X)$.
This can be accomplished by taking the atoms over $occurs/2$ from $M$ as facts together with a program $C$ containing the facts representing a planning task
and the following encoding:
\begin{listing}[H]
  \caption{Encoding for checking circular invalidation}
  \inputminted[frame=single,framesep=2mm,baselinestretch=1.2,linenos]{prolog}{encodings/EX_check.lp}
  \label{check}
\end{listing}
\noindent Here, the maximum value for $t$ in subprogram $check$ and $step$ is $m = max \{t \mid occurs(a, t)\}$ and
$C$ becomes $unsatisfiable$ if $t > m$.
Like in Listing \ref{exists}, we check for each $t$ if all actions $A \in occurs(A,t)$ can be applied by the predicate $ready$ (Line 7-11).
If actions in $A$ circularly invalidate each other, $ready(A,t)$ is underivable for these actions and thus $cycle(t)$ (Line 12) in step $t$ is derived.
Hence, $C$ becomes satisfiable if there is a circular invalidation in step $t$, which means that there is no permutation of the actions A, s.t. the successor state is defined 
and therefore not (relaxed) $\exists$-step serializable.


\subsection{Heuristic}
The general idea of heuristics in clingo described by \parencite{DIMOPOULOS_2019} is to extend the search heuristic by giving each atom a
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{level} ($\in \mathbb Z$, $0$ by default), allowing to modify the order of which atoms are selected first
  \item \textit{sign} ($\in \mathbb Z$, $0$ by default, where values $>0$ corresponds to $true$, $<0$ to $false$ and $0$ to $undefined$), which allows for controlling the truth value assigned to atoms. 
\end{itemize}
During solving, these values get modified during the activation of \textbf{\#heuristic} directives.
The search heuristic of clingo selects the atom with the highest level and sets it to its associated \textit{sign}, if \textit{sign} defined, else switching to the default \textit{sign} heuristic.

\begin{listing}[H]
  \caption{backward progpagating heuristic}
  \inputminted[frame=single,framesep=2mm,baselinestretch=1.2,linenos]{prolog}{encodings/heuristic.lp}
  \label{heuristic}
\end{listing}

\noindent The heuristic in Listing \ref{heuristic} aims starting at the goal of a planning task and then searching backwards.
Here both lines have the level $l=2^{31}-1-t$ where $2^{31}-1$ is the largest integer supported by clingo, giving the starting steps the highest priority.
The second line expresses that whenever clingo made $holds(X,V,t)$ true, $holds(X,V,t-1)$ should be made true at the level $l$ as well.
Similarly, the directive in Line 3 is actived when an instance of $holds(X,V,t)$ becomes false, in which case $holds(X,V,t-1)$ should be made false at level $l$ as well.
Both directives associates atoms over holds/3 with higher level the earlier the state is, i.e. the value for $t$ is smaller, 
which intends to bias the search of \textit{clingo} to establish the goal conditions as early as possible. 
\section{Experiment}
To compare the different planning encodings and the effectiveness of the optimizations, we run four experiments with the pipeline described above.
Each experiment run on the same set of benchmark instances with the sequential, $\forall$-step, \textit{relaxed} $\exists$-step, \textit{guess and check} and both versions of the $\exists$-step encodings.
The Guess and Check algorithm and the fallback described above are started one after the other with the time limit of 30 minutes, where the times are added together if the check fails and the $\forall$-step encoding finds a solution. 
If the summed up time is below the time limit, the problem is considered solved. \\
Aborted runs are considered unsolved and assigned the time nan.
\textit{Cpddl} is equipped with and without the h2 option that extends the seach for mutexes and the planning encodings with and without the backward propagating heuristic. 
The experiments were run on the bwicluster3.0 server of the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT) using nodes with Intel Xeon Gold 6248R CPUs (3.0 GHz, 24 cores).
Each run was limited to one CPU core, where \textit{clingo} and \textit{cpddl} were given a time limit of 30 minutes and a memory limit of 32 GiB each.

\subsection{Coverage Analysis}
The coverage results in Figure reveal several distinct trends regarding the effectiveness of parallel planning.

\paragraph{Sequential vs. Parallel:}
In domains that inherently allow for a high degree of concurrency, parallel encodings significantly outperform the sequential approach. 
Notable examples include \textit{airport} and \textit{elevators}. In \textit{airport}, the sequential encoding (S) solves only 11 out of 50 instances, whereas the $\forall$-step encoding (V) solves 49/50 and the $\exists$-step variants solve 48/50. 
Similarly, in \textit{floortile}, the sequential approach fails to solve any instance (0/40), while the standard $\exists$-step encoding solves 37/40. This confirms that the reduced time horizon $T$ of parallel plans effectively mitigates the search depth explosion found in sequential planning.

\paragraph{Comparison of Parallel Semantics:}
Comparing the different parallel semantics yields interesting insights into the trade-off between plan flexibility and encoding complexity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$\forall$-step (V):} Despite being the most restrictive parallel semantic, the $\forall$-step encoding performs surprisingly well in many domains (e.g., \textit{parcprinter}, \textit{woodworking}). This suggests that many IPC domains do not require complex interleaved actions, and the simpler encoding of $\forall$-step (avoiding quadratic constraints) puts less burden on the grounder and solver.
    \item \textbf{$\exists$-step ($\exists$ / E):} The $\exists$-step semantics show their strength in domains with complex dependencies. A prime example is \textit{floortile}, where $\forall$-step only solves 18/40 instances, but $\exists$-step solves 37/40. This indicates that the flexibility of $\exists$-step allows for finding plans in restricted environments where $\forall$-step fails. The alternative encoding (E) generally performs on par with the standard $\exists$-step, showing no significant deviation in coverage.
    \item \textbf{Relaxed $\exists$-step (R):} The relaxed encoding typically solves fewer instances than the standard $\exists$-step (e.g., \textit{freecell}: 9 vs. 14). While theoretically allowing shorter plans, the relaxed encoding likely increases the search space or grounding size, leading to resource exhaustion on harder instances.
\end{itemize}

The runtime heatmaps in Figure  corroborate the coverage findings. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item **Efficiency of Parallelism:** In domains where parallel planning is superior in coverage, it is also drastically faster.
